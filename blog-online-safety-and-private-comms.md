# The Surveillance Bargain Hidden Inside Online Safety Laws

*SCD Hub · Exotopia.org · June 2026*

---

We built a small commenting system for our blog. Seven people can leave notes for each other on posts about exoplanets, coastal cleanup work, and lattice cryptography. Before any two people can see each other's words, both must explicitly agree to connect. There is no public feed. There is no algorithm deciding what you see. There is no company reading your messages.

Under the laws currently in force in Australia and the United Kingdom, and the law currently being debated in the United States House of Representatives, this system may be illegal.

Not because it does anything harmful. But because it does not do enough to prove that it might not.

---

## What These Laws Actually Require

The Australian Online Safety Act 2021, the UK's Online Safety Act 2023, and the proposed US Kids Online Safety Act (KOSA) are all animated by a genuine and serious problem: children are being harmed online. The harms are real — eating disorder content algorithmically amplified to teenagers, sextortion, coordinated harassment, commercial platforms optimised to keep vulnerable users engaged regardless of the psychological cost.

These are not invented concerns. The research on algorithmic harm to adolescent mental health is substantial, if contested in some specifics.

But watch what happens when a legislature tries to write a law around this problem. The regulator needs to define who the law applies to. Every definition they use turns out to be too broad or too narrow — usually both simultaneously, in different directions.

**Australia:** any service where users can post content that may be accessible to other users. Our commenting system, a private Discord server, a family WhatsApp group chat hosted on a web interface — all potentially captured.

**United Kingdom:** any "user-to-user service" where content generated by one user can be "encountered" by another. The distinction between a private closed group and a public social network does not appear in the definition. Duty of care applies to both; the question is only how much.

**KOSA (US Senate-passed version, 2024):** online platforms that provide features including "comments, reactions, and interactive functionality" — where the operator has actual knowledge that minors are using the service, or the platform is "designed and marketed for minors." The interaction features language is strikingly broad.

Once you are captured by these definitions, the obligations cascade. You must prevent minors from encountering harmful content. To prevent something, you must detect it. To detect it reliably at scale, you use automated systems. Automated systems that read private communications are surveillance systems. The law does not use that word, but the mechanism is the same.

---

## The Proportionality Problem

Every regulator will tell you the obligations are "proportionate" to the platform's size and capacity. This is true in the letter and inadequate in the spirit.

Proportionality means a small platform like ours has lighter duties than Meta. It does not mean we have no duties. The duty exists. What is "proportionate" for us to do to detect harmful content in private conversations between seven adults who have each consented to communicate with each other?

Under a strict reading, the answer is: something. What that something is has not been defined. But the pressure of potential liability pushes toward more scanning, not less. A platform that gets it wrong can face civil penalties (Australia), enforcement notices (UK), FTC action (US). The safe harbour tends toward surveillance as a defensive posture.

This is the mechanism by which online safety laws that nobody opposes in principle produce surveillance infrastructure that erodes the privacy rights of everyone on the platform, not just the children they were designed to protect.

## Our Position — and Its Honest Complications

We designed our system to prohibit AI audit of member communications. Both because we believe in user privacy and because our platform serves communities where surveillance is not an abstract concern.

A field worker in coastal Kenya documenting a pollution source for a corporate cleanup report operates in a context where that communication, if intercepted, could end her livelihood or worse. An indigenous land rights monitor in the Amazon basin documenting illegal deforestation is not a hypothetical case — people doing this work are killed. A community banking group in Mpeketoni sharing financial information in a digital forum operates in a context where that data has historically been extracted and used against them.

Our no-AI-audit principle is not about protecting trolls or bad actors. It is about the communities we actually serve.

But we have to be honest about where this principle runs into hard limits.

**We will implement CSAM detection.** Child sexual abuse material is illegal everywhere, including in the communities we serve. Hash-matching against known illegal image databases (the National Center for Missing and Exploited Children's database, the Internet Watch Foundation's hash list) is not AI reading of communications — it is comparing file hashes against a list of known illegal files. This is not surveillance. We will implement it when image uploads are supported.

**We will comply with targeted lawful orders.** If a court or competent authority serves us with a legally valid production order for the communications of a specific user suspected of a serious crime, we will comply with what the law requires. We will not maintain bulk surveillance infrastructure that pre-positions us to comply with mass data demands.

**We cannot promise that our proportionality argument will prevail in every jurisdiction.** It is a reasonable argument. It is a defensible argument. It is not a guaranteed one.

## The Instance Operator Problem

We are an open-source project. Our code is GPL v3 — anyone can fork it, run it, and build communities on it. This is intentional and important. Decentralisation is part of our privacy architecture.

It creates a genuine ethical complication we have not fully resolved.

When an operator in, say, the Philippines runs an instance of Exotopia's commenting system for a community of local environmental monitors, they become a platform operator. They inherit every obligation we have described in this document, for their jurisdiction. Philippine online safety law is evolving. Indonesian law has its own contours. Each operator needs to understand what they are signing up for.

We cannot resolve this by writing good documentation, though we are writing it. We cannot resolve it by including compliance hooks in the codebase, though we are building them. The honest answer is that decentralisation distributes both the power and the responsibility, and smaller operators have fewer resources to carry the compliance burden.

This is not a reason not to build open-source tools. It is a reason to be honest about what open-source cannot solve alone.

## KOSA Specifically — Why We Are Lobbying Against It

SCD Hub is a Colorado-incorporated organisation. We are in the congressional district represented by Joe Neguse (D-CO-02), a member of the House Judiciary Committee. We are asking Rep. Neguse to oppose KOSA in its current form.

We want to be precise about what we are asking for and why, because "oppose KOSA" can mean different things.

**We are not arguing that children do not need better online protection.** They do. The evidence is sufficient and the urgency is real.

**We are not arguing that platforms should have no obligations.** They should. Commercial platforms that have built billion-dollar businesses on the behavioral data of minors bear direct responsibility for the infrastructure they created.

**We are arguing that KOSA's mechanism is wrong.** The "duty of care" framework, as currently drafted, creates a liability structure that incentivises surveillance over privacy, encourages over-removal of protected speech, and threatens to make end-to-end encryption legally untenable for small providers who cannot afford the legal risk of maintaining it in the face of a vague "duty to prevent harm."

**We are arguing that the wrong platforms bear the cost.** KOSA's obligations would fall most heavily on small operators who lack the legal, engineering, and compliance resources of the companies that created the problem. Facebook and TikTok have compliance departments. We have this document.

**What we believe works better:**

*Modernise COPPA, don't replace it with duty of care.* The Children's Online Privacy Protection Act already gives the FTC authority over children's data. Strengthening and expanding it — harder data minimisation requirements, meaningful consent standards, prohibition of behavioral advertising to minors, real enforcement budgets — addresses the commercial surveillance infrastructure that is the actual source of algorithmic harm.

*Prescriptive design standards instead of vague duty of care.* Tell platforms specifically what they cannot do: no algorithmic amplification of certain content categories for accounts flagged as minor, no surveillance-based advertising to anyone under 18, no dark patterns for subscription cancellation. Precise prohibitions are enforceable and do not require platforms to guess what surveillance will satisfy a regulator.

*Protect mutual consent private communications explicitly.* Any online safety legislation should include a clear carve-out for closed, mutual-consent communication tools — analogous to the existing legal treatment of private correspondence. A private letter between two adults who chose to write to each other is not regulated the same way a broadcast is. Digital private messaging should not be either.

*Fund digital literacy instead of surveilling children's communications.* A dollar spent teaching teenagers to critically evaluate algorithmic content, to understand how their behavioral data is used, and to set up and use private communications tools is more protective than a dollar spent scanning their messages.

## The Broader Ethical Quandary

We are a small project. We build tools for community ecological monitoring in coastal Kenya, for solar water disinfection training in Central America, for indigenous knowledge documentation in the Amazon. We built a blog comment system so that seven colleagues can discuss our work.

We did not build a social network. We built a very small room where a few people who have chosen to trust each other can talk.

That room is now legally ambiguous in three of the largest regulatory jurisdictions on Earth. Not because it does anything harmful. But because it looks, from a regulatory distance, like a shape that other rooms — rooms that have caused real harm — also make.

This is the surveillance bargain hidden inside well-intentioned safety legislation: the mechanism used to detect harm in large systems, applied at scale, becomes a mechanism for monitoring all private communication, everywhere.

We are not willing to accept that bargain. We are willing to accept real obligations: CSAM detection, lawful targeted orders, transparent practices, age appropriate design choices. We are not willing to build surveillance infrastructure as the price of operating a private communication tool between consenting adults.

The law, in several places, has not yet decided whether we are allowed to make that choice. We think we should be. We are going to keep making it while the law catches up.
