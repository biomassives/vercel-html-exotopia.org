# Eco Ops Workflow Guide

## Managing Community Environmental Projects from Site to Library

**SCD Hub / Exotopia.org — Version 1.0 — June 2026**

---

## Overview

An eco op is any community-led action at a physical location that produces ecological benefit, a documentable outcome, and learnable knowledge. This guide covers the full workflow for running eco ops through the SCD Hub platform — from identifying and securing a site, to delivering the work, to archiving what was learned in a form that accumulates value over time.

The guide is organised by project type. Each section covers: what the project is, how to assess and secure a site for it, what the workflow looks like in the field, how to document outcomes, and how to connect the record to the platform's expanding library. A final section covers the Intersectional Curricula Framework — the approach that links technical project work to elder knowledge, endangered languages, and traditional ecological practice.

The six core eco op types covered here are:

1. PFOS/PFAS Monitoring
2. Forest Gardening
3. Rain Gardens
4. Acopian Bird Blinds (window-strike prevention)
5. Endangered Language and Knowledge Documentation
6. Wisdom from Elders — Intersectional Curricula

## Part 1: Securing Locations

Before any eco op work begins, the site must be secured. "Secured" means: the property owner or manager has given documented consent, the liability position is understood, the site is physically accessible to participants, and the scope of permitted work is agreed.

### 1.1 Site Types and Typical Pathway to Securing

**School grounds and schoolyards**  
Contact: building principal → facilities director → district sustainability or science coordinator  
What's needed: a brief project description (one page), a faculty sponsor, and a parent consent form template for students under 18. Many districts have a standing MOU template for university or nonprofit partners. If working with state extension (land-grant university), use the extension's liability umbrella where available.  
Useful angle: grant-matching. Many district science departments can access STEM grants for which a university or nonprofit partner is a qualifying co-applicant.

**Public parks and greenways**  
Contact: local parks and recreation department or county open space program  
What's needed: volunteer activity permit (most jurisdictions: free or nominal fee, 2–4 weeks lead time); evidence of liability insurance for the organising group (SCD Hub can provide a COI letter for affiliated groups); site map showing the work zone  
Note: invasive species removal and planting (relevant to forest gardening and rain gardens) often require a separate vegetation management permit; check with the parks department.

**Municipal stormwater infrastructure and road rights-of-way**  
Contact: city or county public works / stormwater management division  
Relevant for: rain garden installations in the public right-of-way (between sidewalk and street); PFAS monitoring near stormwater outfalls  
What's needed: an encroachment permit or right-of-way agreement; municipal stormwater programs in many cities are actively seeking partners for green infrastructure installation and may have a formal community partnership pathway. Contact the stormwater program manager, not the general public works line.

**Private land (farms, large private lots, institutional campuses)**  
Contact: property owner or estate manager; for universities and institutions, the facilities or grounds manager  
What's needed: a written agreement (even an informal letter of agreement is better than a verbal one) covering: permitted activities, duration, access hours, who is responsible for participant safety, who owns any physical improvements, and what happens to monitoring equipment left on site  
Template: see Appendix A for a minimal letter-of-agreement template  
Note: for PFAS monitoring or any work that generates data about potential contamination on private land, discuss data ownership and publication rights explicitly before beginning. Some landowners will be concerned about liability if monitoring data suggests contamination — this is a legitimate concern that must be addressed transparently.

**Tribal lands and territories**  
Contact: tribal environmental department or tribal council  
What's needed: formal FPIC process (see Endangered Language section and compliance/SOCIAL-COMMENTARY-ONLINE-SAFETY.md); the tribe is the lead, not SCD Hub; any data collected on tribal land belongs to the tribe; certificates co-signed by tribal environmental office, not by SCD Hub alone  
This process takes longer and is more involved than other site types — plan for 3–6 months of relationship-building before field work begins. The depth of partnership this produces is worth the time.

**Community gardens and food forests**  
Contact: garden committee or nonprofit land trust managing the space  
These are typically the easiest sites to secure for forest gardening and rain garden work because the site managers are already oriented toward ecological benefit. Bring a site assessment and a plant-guild proposal. Community gardens are excellent for intersectional curricula work because they often already have multi-generational participants.

### 1.2 Site Assessment Checklist (All Project Types)

Before committing to a site, document the following:

- [ ] Property owner/manager name, contact, and written consent pathway
- [ ] Site map with dimensions and existing features (buildings, utilities, drainage)
- [ ] Soil drainage: percolation test result (dig 12" hole, fill with water, time to drain; >24 hrs indicates poor drainage — important for rain gardens and forest gardening)
- [ ] Canopy coverage estimate (% of site in shade at noon in summer)
- [ ] Proximity to water (distance to nearest stream, stormwater inlet, or drinking water well — important for PFAS, rain garden placement, and macroinvertebrate monitoring)
- [ ] Utility line check — call 811 (US national "call before you dig") before any ground disturbance; required by law
- [ ] Existing vegetation: invasive species present? (document before removal begins)
- [ ] Historical land use: was this site previously industrial, agricultural, or military? (Flag for PFAS pre-screening)
- [ ] Wildlife: nesting birds, protected species habitat? Check state natural heritage program database before disturbing vegetation.
- [ ] Access: wheelchair/mobility access for participants? Shade and water available for summer work?

## Part 2: PFOS/PFAS Monitoring

### What it is

Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) are a class of approximately 12,000 synthetic chemicals characterised by extremely strong carbon-fluorine bonds. They do not break down in the environment and accumulate in human tissue. PFOS (perfluorooctane sulfonic acid) is among the most studied PFAS compounds and among the most widely detected in US water supplies. EPA established Maximum Contaminant Levels for six PFAS compounds in drinking water in 2024 — the first federal regulatory limits.

PFAS contamination in drinking water is heavily concentrated near:
- Military bases and airfields that used AFFF (aqueous film-forming foam) for fire training
- Manufacturing plants that produced or used PFAS (Teflon/PTFE manufacturing, stain-resistant coating application)
- Airports with AFFF fire suppression
- Landfills receiving PFAS-containing waste

On the East Coast, documented hotspots include the area around Pease Air Force Base (NH), Joint Base Cape Cod / Otis ANGB (MA), Joint Base McGuire-Dix-Lakehurst (NJ), and Willow Grove Naval Air Station (PA). The Environmental Working Group (EWG) maintains a continuously updated PFAS contamination map at ewg.org/interactive-maps/pfas_contamination.

### Site selection for community monitoring

Community PFAS monitoring is most impactful in two scenarios:

1. **Unmonitored private wells** near known contamination sources — particularly in rural areas where households rely on shallow groundwater and are not covered by EPA's UCMR5 (Unregulated Contaminant Monitoring Rule), which applied only to public water systems with ≥25 service connections
2. **Surface water near suspected sources** — streams and ponds downstream of bases, airports, or industrial sites where AFFF discharge or PFAS-containing runoff may have occurred

### Monitoring workflow

1. **Pre-screening** — use EWG's PFAS map and state environmental agency GIS data to identify proximity to known sources. Document the site's distance from the nearest source, prevailing wind/drainage direction, and land use history.

2. **Sample collection** — use EPA-certified collection protocol (EPA Method 533 or 537.1 compatible containers — order from Pace Analytical, TestAmerica, or your state's certified lab list; labs will ship pre-cleaned containers with instructions). Collect at least 250mL from the tap (after 5-minute flush) for drinking water, or from the stream at mid-current, mid-depth with no turbulence for surface water. Label with GPS coordinates, date/time, sample ID.

3. **Chain of custody** — fill out the lab's chain-of-custody form completely; this is what makes the result legally defensible. Keep a photo copy. Ship on ice in a cooler within 24 hours of collection.

4. **Cost** — expect $150–$400 per sample for a full PFAS panel. Some state health departments offer subsidised testing for private well owners near known contamination sites — check with your state health department's drinking water program before paying full lab price.

5. **Platform documentation** — create a site record in the platform with:
   - GPS coordinates of sample point
   - Upstream contamination source (type, distance)
   - Sample date and lab ID
   - Result: PFAS detected (list compounds and concentrations) or not detected
   - EPA MCL comparison: flag any compound exceeding 4 ppt for PFOA or PFOS, 10 ppt for PFNA or PFHxS, etc.
   - Access tier: community-controlled (results visible to the contributing community group and to health authorities they designate; not publicly searchable by default)

6. **Follow-up** — if results exceed EPA MCLs or state standards: notify the state drinking water program, the EPA regional office, and — for impacted residents — their local health department. The Safe Drinking Water Hotline is 800-426-4791. For private well owners, a point-of-entry filter certified for PFAS (reverse osmosis or activated carbon at rated volume) is an immediate protective measure while the regulatory response proceeds.

### Certificate issued

`eco:monitoring — PFAS Community Water Testing` — co-signed by the organising group; includes sample location (generalised to protect homeowner privacy), date, and reference to the platform record

## Part 3: Forest Gardening

### What it is

Forest gardening — also called food forestry or permaculture agroforestry — is the cultivation of perennial food-producing systems that mimic the structure of a natural woodland. A forest garden is designed across seven layers: canopy (large fruit and nut trees), sub-canopy (smaller fruit trees, nitrogen-fixers), shrub (berry shrubs, medicinal plants), herbaceous (perennial vegetables and herbs), groundcover (living mulch, low-growing edibles), root zone (root vegetables, bulbs), and vine (climbing plants on supporting trees). Once established, a forest garden produces food with dramatically less labour than annual vegetable gardening and provides wildlife habitat, carbon sequestration, and stormwater infiltration.

### Site selection for a community forest garden

- Minimum viable area: ~200m² (a long, narrow strip 5m × 40m works well for an urban site)
- Sun: south-facing, minimum 6 hours direct sun for a productive canopy; north-facing slopes work for shade-tolerant species but limit yield
- Soil: test pH (target 6.0–7.0 for most species); check for lead contamination on urban sites (raised beds or bioremediation with sunflowers and comfrey if lead >400 ppm)
- Water: the site should hold moisture reasonably well; clay-heavy soils actually work well for deep-rooted perennials once initial compaction is broken

### Workflow

**Year 0 — Assessment and Design:**
- Map existing trees, slope, drainage, and sun angles
- Soil test (pH, NPK, organic matter, lead if urban)
- Community design session: what plants do people want to grow? What local traditional food plants should be represented? (This is the entry point for intersectional curricula — what did people grow here before?)
- Identify a guild structure: what canopy tree anchors each guild? (Mulberry, pear, apple, chestnut, persimmon, or pawpaw depending on region)
- Document the design in the platform site record with a georeferenced sketch map

**Year 0 — Installation:**
- 811 call → utility clearance
- Sheet mulch entire planting area (6–8 layers cardboard, 15–20cm wood chip mulch on top) to suppress existing vegetation without soil disturbance — this is the single most important first step
- Plant canopy trees first; space at 5–8m centres; stake and protect from deer browse (tree tubes on new plantings for first 2–3 years)
- Plant nitrogen-fixing companion species (Siberian pea shrub, alder, locust, comfrey) around canopy trees
- Let shrub and herbaceous layers fill in through Year 2–3

**Ongoing documentation (platform record):**
- Annual photo transect from fixed points (mark with rebar stakes in Year 0)
- Species inventory update each spring
- Yield record: what was harvested, by whom, in what quantity — this is the community value metric
- Soil test every 3 years (compare to Year 0 baseline)
- Pest and disease observations

**Certificate issued:** `eco:habitat — Community Forest Garden, [Site Name]` — co-signed by the land partner; includes plant guild map, photo transect, and species inventory as supporting evidence

## Part 4: Rain Gardens

### What it is

A rain garden is a shallow planted depression designed to receive stormwater runoff — from a roof, driveway, or lawn — and allow it to infiltrate into the ground over 24–48 hours rather than running directly into storm drains and waterways. Rain gardens remove 80–90% of nutrients, 90% of sediment, and 60–80% of pathogens from stormwater before it reaches groundwater. They are one of the most effective individual-scale actions against lake eutrophication and combined sewer overflow.

A well-designed rain garden typically handles the runoff from a 1,000–2,000 sq ft impervious area (one rooftop) through a 50–150 sq ft garden. The math is forgiving — oversizing a rain garden is not a problem.

### Site selection

- **10-foot minimum** from any building foundation (prevents basement infiltration)
- **Percolation test required** — if water doesn't drain within 24–48 hours, the site needs amendment or is unsuitable (a French drain connection can sometimes resolve this)
- **No utility conflicts** — 811 call before excavation
- Not in wetland or 100-year floodplain (these areas already function as sponges; a rain garden there is redundant and may be regulated)
- Visible from a path or gathering space if possible — a well-placed rain garden is a teaching object; people who see it ask what it is

### Workflow

1. **Site delineation** — identify the downslope from the target impervious surface; survey the grade with a line level; mark the garden footprint with spray paint
2. **Excavation** — dig to 6–12" depth (deeper on sites with poor drainage); set aside topsoil; remove any compacted subsoil layer
3. **Bioretention media** — fill with 50–60% sand, 20–30% compost, 20% existing topsoil (this ratio balances drainage speed with nutrient retention); spread to 8" depth
4. **Inlet and overflow engineering** — the inlet (where water enters) receives a splash pad (large gravel or flat stone) to prevent erosion; the overflow (where water exits if the garden fills) must be directed away from foundations; use a notched berm or perforated overflow pipe
5. **Planting** — native plants are strongly preferred: they are adapted to the local drought/flood cycle and support native pollinators. East Coast species: switchgrass (*Panicum virgatum*), swamp milkweed (*Asclepias incarnata*), cardinal flower (*Lobelia cardinalis*), blue flag iris (*Iris versicolor*), coneflower (*Echinacea purpurea*), buttonbush (*Cephalanthus occidentalis*) for wet zones, native sedges (*Carex* spp.) for ground cover. Avoid invasive species — do not use purple loosestrife or Japanese iris regardless of their aesthetic appeal.
6. **Mulch** — 3–4" of shredded hardwood mulch (not dyed, not cypress — use locally sourced hardwood); keep mulch back from plant crowns
7. **Establishment watering** — water deeply twice a week for the first growing season; rain gardens are drought-stressed in their first summer before root systems develop

**Platform documentation:**
- Before photo, construction photo, after-planting photo
- GPS coordinates and site dimensions
- Drainage area (what impervious surface feeds it)
- Plant list with installation date
- Estimated stormwater capture volume (calculation: garden area × 0.3 ft effective depth × 0.3 fill ratio for annual average = estimated cubic feet/year)
- Year 1 and Year 3 follow-up photo for success documentation

**Certificate issued:** `eco:infrastructure — Rain Garden Installation, [Site Name]`  
**Connection to eutrophication unit:** the nutrient source map from the Cyanobacteria Watch educational unit can directly identify rain garden placement sites. Encourage groups to do both.

## Part 5: Acopian Bird Blinds

### What it is

Window collisions are the second-largest human-caused source of bird mortality in the United States after outdoor cats — estimated at 600 million birds per year. The cause is glass that is either transparent (birds attempt to fly through) or reflective (birds fly toward the reflected sky or vegetation). Most collisions happen at residential and small commercial buildings, not skyscrapers.

Acopian BirdSavers are external paracord curtains — lengths of 550 paracord hung vertically on window exteriors at 10cm (4") spacing. The American Bird Conservancy standard is: no gap wider than 5cm (2") × 10cm (4"). At that density, birds recognise the surface as an obstacle rather than open space. The installation takes 30–60 minutes per window with basic tools and costs approximately $5–$15 per window depending on window size and paracord source.

### Site selection and strike assessment

Not all windows are equally dangerous. A strike survey identifies where to focus resources:

**Strike indicators:**
- Bird body imprints (feather prints, grease marks) on glass
- Dead or stunned birds found under windows
- Feathers on the ground below
- Seasonal concentration (spring and fall migration — April/May and September/October — are peak strike periods when migratory birds are moving through unfamiliar areas)

**High-risk window characteristics:**
- Glass facing vegetation (a bird sees the reflected tree as flyable-through space)
- Pass-through windows (two aligned windows on opposite sides of a building — birds see "sky" through the building)
- Windows near bird feeders at 1–3m distance (too close for birds to avoid after flush; farther than 10m is safer)
- Corner glass
- Glass that reflects sky (horizontal or angled glass panels)

**Strike survey protocol:**
- Walk the building perimeter on two separate occasions (morning preferred)
- Record location, species (if identifiable), and date of each strike or evidence found
- Photograph feather prints before they wash away
- Submit to Fatal Light Awareness Program (FLAP) database or eBird mortality report
- Document pre-installation baseline in platform site record

### Installation workflow

1. Measure window width at top; subtract 2cm; this is the cord-span
2. Install eye hooks or adhesive cord anchors at top of window frame or sill at 10cm intervals
3. Cut paracord lengths to window height + 15cm; melt ends to prevent fraying
4. Attach loops at top; let lengths hang freely
5. No lower attachment needed — cords that swing freely are fine; cords that are weighted fall more uniformly
6. Alternative for rental or historic buildings: external suction cup anchor systems; or internal window film (ABC-certified dot pattern at 6.35cm spacing) if external installation is prohibited

**Post-installation monitoring:**
- Wait three migration seasons before concluding effectiveness (year-one sample is often too small)
- Continue strike survey on treated windows; compare to untreated control windows on same building
- Species documentation: if migratory species are being struck, submit records to state ornithological society or Cornell Lab eBird

**Platform documentation:**
- Before/after photos of windows treated
- Strike count by month: pre-installation (minimum 2 seasons if possible), post-installation
- Species list of struck birds (if IDable) — this is valuable migratory pattern data
- GPS of building with orientation (which compass direction does the dangerous glass face?)

**Certificate issued:** `eco:habitat — Bird-Safe Building, [Building Name]` — co-signed by building owner or facility manager; includes strike survey data as supporting evidence

**Connections:** Bird-friendly building standards connect to LEED certification (credit available for light pollution reduction and bird-safe glazing), urban bird monitoring programs (CBC — Christmas Bird Count, GBBC — Great Backyard Bird Count, eBird), and school campus greening programs.

## Part 6: Endangered Language and Knowledge Documentation

### What it is

An estimated 3,000 of the world's approximately 7,000 languages are at risk of disappearing within a generation. Language loss is not only a cultural loss — it is an ecological loss. Indigenous and community languages encode centuries of observational knowledge about local species, seasonal timing, land management practices, weather patterns, and ecological relationships that frequently has no translation equivalent in dominant languages. When the language dies, that knowledge becomes inaccessible even to the descendants of the communities that developed it.

SCD Hub's Endangered Language Documentation eco op type provides a structured workflow for:
- Identifying languages in the network's geographic areas that are threatened or endangered
- Connecting with elder speakers and knowledge holders
- Recording and archiving knowledge in appropriate formats with community-controlled access
- Integrating recorded knowledge into the platform's library and into intersectional curricula

### Before beginning: FPIC and community governance

All language documentation work requires Free, Prior and Informed Consent from the speaker community and its governance structure — not just from individual speakers. This is a non-negotiable requirement reflecting both legal obligations (ILO C169, UNDRIP Article 31) and ethical best practice in linguistic documentation.

**What FPIC requires in practice:**
- A meeting with the community governance body (council, elders' committee, language program director) before any recording begins — not with individual speakers who may not have authority to speak for the community's knowledge commons
- A written consent framework that describes: what will be recorded, how recordings will be stored, who will have access to the archive (community-only, partner researchers, general public, or tiered), what happens to the archive if SCD Hub ceases operations, whether speakers have the right to withdraw consent and have recordings removed
- Ongoing check-in — consent is not a one-time event; if a speaker or community changes their mind, recordings are removed from public-accessible tiers immediately

**Data sovereignty:** All recordings, transcripts, and associated metadata are owned by the contributing community. SCD Hub holds only a licensed copy for the purposes described in the consent framework. Community-controlled access tiers are enforced in the platform library.

### Recording workflow

**Equipment:**
- Minimum quality for archival deposit: 48kHz / 24-bit WAV audio
- Recommended: external directional microphone (Rode VideoMicro or similar) connected to a smartphone or field recorder; smartphone internal microphones are acceptable for informal sessions but not for archival master recordings
- Video: useful for documenting sign language, gesture, and physical demonstration of knowledge (e.g., how to prepare a traditional plant medicine — the physical process may not be fully described in words alone)
- Backup: record to two devices simultaneously; audio is irreplaceable after the session

**Session structure:**
- Begin with the speaker describing themselves in their own language: name, community, the language or dialect they are speaking — this becomes the metadata header
- Structure sessions by domain rather than topic jumping: one session for place names and geographic knowledge; one session for plant knowledge; one session for seasonal calendar; one session for ecological observations and memory
- Allow the speaker to direct — follow their associations, not a rigid interview protocol
- Leave space: silences in oral narrative are meaningful; don't fill them
- At the end of each session, review with the speaker what was covered and confirm what access tier applies to each segment

**Annotation:**
- Use ELAN (Eudico Linguistic Annotator — free, developed by MPI Nijmegen) for time-aligned transcription and translation
- Minimum annotation: a word-level transcription and a free English (or relevant dominant language) translation
- Tag each segment with domain labels: `[place]` `[plant]` `[animal]` `[season]` `[practice]` `[memory]` `[story]`
- Link `[plant]` and `[animal]` annotations to the iNaturalist taxon ID where a species correspondence exists

**Archival deposit:**
- ELAR (Endangered Languages Archive, SOAS University of London) is the international standard archival repository; free for community deposits; access tiers enforceable by the community
- Alternative: AILLA (Archive of Indigenous Languages of Latin America) for Central/South American languages; PARADISEC for Pacific and Southeast Asian languages
- SCD Hub platform library holds a linked record pointing to the archival deposit, with the access tier metadata; it does not hold a redundant copy unless explicitly requested by the community

### Certificate issued

`eco:indigenous — Language Documentation Contributor, [Language Name]` — co-signed by community governance body; knowledge holder named with their consent; archival deposit reference included

## Part 7: Intersectional Curricula Framework — Wisdom from Elders

### The connective tissue

Every eco op site has a human history. The forest garden being planted this year is on land where something else grew before — and someone remembers what. The lake being monitored for cyanobacteria was swum in by someone who watched it change. The stream corridor being assessed for macroinvertebrates was fished by someone's grandmother who had names for the seasonal patterns of water and insect and fish that exist in no monitoring report.

The Intersectional Curricula Framework is the methodology for connecting technical eco op work to this prior human knowledge. It is not a separate program — it is a lens applied to all eco ops work.

### Core principle: elder observation is primary data

An elder who has lived near a monitoring site for sixty years has a longitudinal dataset that no academic sampling program can replicate. Their observations — communicated in natural language, tied to personal memory, expressed through the language and knowledge system of their community — are scientifically valuable as:

- **Baseline data**: what was the water clarity in 1965? How many deer were there in 1980? When did ticks start appearing in this neighbourhood?
- **Phenological records**: when did the ice go out? When did the first peepers sing? When did the caddisflies hatch?
- **Species presence/absence**: what species were here that aren't now? What shows up now that was never here before?
- **Land use history**: what happened on this land before the current use? Who used it, for what?

This data belongs in the monitoring record alongside the Secchi disk readings and the macroinvertebrate kick-sample counts.

### Integration workflow

**Step 1 — Identify Knowledge Keepers at the site**  
Before or during the site assessment, ask: who has been here the longest? Is there an elder in the community, neighbourhood, or tribal group with direct knowledge of this site? Is there a garden club, historical society, or community memory project that has already collected some of this knowledge?

**Step 2 — Introduce the documentation framework**  
Explain what you are trying to do: you want their memory to be part of the site record, alongside the biological data. You want to document it in a form that persists, with whatever level of attribution and access they are comfortable with.

**Step 3 — Structured knowledge session**  
This does not require the formal endangered language protocol unless the speaker's primary knowledge language is threatened. A conversation in English (or Spanish, or Swahili, or whatever is the speaker's confident language) is the right approach. Use the same domain structure: place, plant, animal, season, practice, memory. Record with permission. ELAN annotation is not required for English-language sessions; a timestamped transcript is sufficient.

**Step 4 — Cross-reference to technical monitoring data**  
Link the elder's observations to the platform's technical record for the site:
- "Elder recalls clear water to 3m depth in the 1970s" → links to the site's Secchi disk record (current depth is 0.8m; this contextualises the change)
- "Elder remembers the first blacklegged ticks appearing in this neighbourhood in the early 1990s" → links to the site's tick index record; confirms range expansion timeline
- "Elder names three plant species in Mohegan that correspond to iNaturalist taxa currently absent from the site" → creates a restoration target list

**Step 5 — Archive and attribute**  
The elder Knowledge Keeper is named in the platform library record with whatever attribution they consent to: full name, first name only, community affiliation, or anonymous. The record notes the date, the eco op site it is linked to, and the domain tags.

### Rewarding elder contributors

Knowledge Keepers receive:
- **`eco:indigenous — Knowledge Keeper, [Site Name]`** certificate — the same credential category used for indigenous ecological knowledge globally; issued with community co-signature where applicable; prominently featured in the site library record
- **Recognition in the platform library** — each Knowledge Keeper's contribution is listed in the site record with a contributor bio (as much or as little as they want to share)
- **Cross-site connections** — the platform library flags when a Knowledge Keeper's observation matches observations at other sites, creating a distributed elder-knowledge network across the SCD Hub field site map

### Intersectional curricula design principles

When designing curricula for any eco op project, the intersectional framework adds three requirements beyond the technical learning outcomes:

1. **Historical layer** — every curriculum unit includes at least one activity anchored in the question "what was this place like before?" This may use elder testimony, historical records, archive photographs, or long-term data comparison.

2. **Multiple knowledge systems** — the curriculum acknowledges that Western scientific monitoring is not the only valid way of knowing the site. Traditional ecological knowledge, oral history, and community memory are presented as complementary methodologies, not as background colour.

3. **Living connection** — at least one Knowledge Keeper is named and credited in the curriculum materials. Students learn the science alongside a named human who has watched the same place their whole life. This is the pedagogical anchor that makes the technical material meaningful.

## Part 8: Library Integration

### How eco ops records feed the platform library

Every eco op, regardless of type, generates a structured record in the platform library. The library is the long-term accumulation of these records — it is what makes a single rain garden installation visible as part of a pattern when viewed alongside twenty other rain garden installations, and what makes a single Knowledge Keeper's observation scientifically legible when cross-referenced with observations at other sites.

### Standard library record structure

Each eco op site record contains:

```
Site ID: [generated by platform]
Site name: [human-readable]
Type: [PFAS | Forest Garden | Rain Garden | Bird Blinds | Language Doc | Elder Knowledge | Citizen Science]
Location: [georeferenced; precision selectable by community — exact or 1km grid]
Access tier: [public | community-restricted | private]

Contributors:
  - [Name/alias], role: [installer | monitor | Knowledge Keeper | SME reviewer], credential: [certificate ID]
  
Technical record:
  - [Type-specific fields: soil test results, species list, strike count, PFAS results, etc.]
  - Photo archive: [IPFS CIDs]
  
Elder knowledge record (if present):
  - Knowledge Keeper: [name/attribution]
  - Recording date: [ISO 8601]
  - Domains: [place | plant | animal | season | practice | memory]
  - Archive deposit: [ELAR/AILLA/PARADISEC record ID]
  - Cross-references: [other sites linked by ecological continuity]

Certificate record:
  - Certificate IDs issued: [list]
  - Co-signing organisations: [list]

Library note:
  - [Free-text summary of what was learned at this site — the "what we discovered here" field, written in plain language for a general audience]
```

### The expanding library

As site records accumulate, the library acquires pattern-level knowledge that no single site record contains:
- Which plant guilds are performing best in which soil types across all forest garden sites
- Where tick nymph densities are highest in which habitat types, cross-referenced with elder accounts of deer population change
- Which waterbodies are on the most accelerated eutrophication trajectory, based on comparative Secchi disk records and nutrient source maps
- Which Knowledge Keepers are observing similar ecological changes independently, from different communities and different language traditions

This is the platform's long-term scientific contribution: not individual site data, but the aggregate picture that emerges from a distributed, protocol-consistent, elder-knowledge-integrated monitoring network.
