# Three Generations, One Forest: A Vision for Biodiversity Collaboration in Costa Rica

**SCD Hub · Exotopia.org · Working draft · GPL v3**
*Intended for release — review local data claims before publishing*

## The country that chose differently

In 1948, Costa Rica disbanded its military. The decision freed up money and political imagination for other things — schools, health, and eventually one of the most ambitious conservation programmes any nation has attempted. Today, more than a quarter of Costa Rica's territory is protected. Roughly 5% of the world's known species live within its borders, in an area smaller than West Virginia.

This did not happen because the forests were left alone. It happened because specific people — campesino farmers who knew which trees held water in the dry season, indigenous communities whose land management had shaped forest structure for centuries, biologists who mapped species ranges and documented what was disappearing — made it happen through specific decisions, over decades.

Those people are ageing. The knowledge they carry is not fully written down. And the generation growing up now — schoolchildren in San José, teenagers in the cloud forest villages around Monteverde, students at the Universidad de Costa Rica — has energy, curiosity, and digital fluency, but is largely disconnected from the ecological knowledge that made Costa Rica's conservation story possible.

This is the gap we think a well-designed community platform can help close.

## What the platform enables

Eco Ops is, at its core, a coordination and recognition system. It connects people who have environmental knowledge with people who want to learn it, structures and records the actions they take together, and issues credentials that the participants can carry forward into schools, grant applications, and professional life.

In the Costa Rica context, that general capability maps onto something specific: a three-way collaboration between elders and skilled practitioners, schools and students, and conservation biologists.

Each group has something the others need. None of them, working alone, can do what all three can do together.

## The three groups and what they bring

### Skilled elders — the living library

Costa Rica's rural communities contain people who have spent fifty years watching a specific forest. They know which fig species fruits first in the dry season and which birds follow it. They know which native trees are hardest to establish in degraded pasture and which species pioneer naturally if you just stop the cattle. They know the old Boruca or Bribri names for plants that the botanical literature catalogued decades later with Latin binomials. They can read a hillside — the colour of the soil, the density of the leaf litter, the presence of certain mosses — and tell you what it was doing ten years ago.

This is not anecdote. This is diachronic ecological knowledge: concentrated on specific places, accumulated across long time periods, qualitative and holistic in a way that short-term quantitative surveys are not. Research on traditional ecological knowledge consistently finds that the most accurate baseline data for restoration after a disturbance event — flood, drought, forest fire — comes from local practitioners who hold historical memory of what the ecosystem looked like before the shock (Berkes, 1993).

The platform's role for this group is recognition and structure. A Knowledge Keeper credential — issued by the platform, co-signed by a partnering institution such as the Universidad Nacional or a SINAC conservation area directorate — acknowledges formally what this knowledge represents. Structured interview guides, recording templates, and consent controls allow knowledgeable elders to contribute to the community knowledge repository on their own terms: choosing what is public, what stays within the community, and what remains private.

A retired nursery worker in Nicoya who has propagated native dry forest species for thirty years should have a certificate that says so — one that a school coordinator, a restoration project manager, or a grant reviewer can verify instantly.

### Schools and students — the activation layer

Costa Rica has a compulsory environmental education component in its school curriculum and a network of schools that span terrain from mangrove coastlines to montane cloud forests. Students in this context are not generic learners. They are geographically situated. The school in Cahuita sits at the edge of Caribbean coastal forest. The school in Cañas sits in the dry Guanacaste lowlands where shade trees along riparian corridors are one of the most effective climate adaptation tools available. The school near Quepos looks toward the Osa Peninsula, one of the most biologically intense places on Earth.

That geographic specificity is an asset the platform can activate. An observation campaign centred on a school's immediate surroundings — a patch of secondary forest behind the football pitch, a stream corridor that runs through town, the trees along the road to the next village — gives students genuine field work with a real audience. The data they collect goes into the community biodiversity record. The species they identify link to iNaturalist, where the international community reviews and confirms identifications. If the campaign is structured correctly, the aggregated dataset can be exported to GBIF with a DOI that the school, the students, and their teachers are named on as contributors.

This matters for retention. Research on school-based citizen science programmes consistently finds that students remain engaged when they have real agency — when they are contributing to something genuine rather than performing data entry — and when there is institutional continuity beyond the individual student's graduation (Posthumus et al., 2020). The platform provides the structure that makes both of those things possible.

The platform issues practicum hour reports, exportable in the format each institution requires. It issues module completion certificates that students can include in university applications. It logs their field contributions to a portfolio they own and can share.

### Conservation biologists — the curriculum backbone

The lesson library is only as good as the science behind it. Costa Rica has a remarkable concentration of conservation biology expertise: researchers at the Universidad de Costa Rica, the Universidad Nacional, CATIE, the Instituto Nacional de Biodiversidad (INBio), and the Tropical Science Center in Monteverde, alongside international institutions that maintain long-term research programmes in places like La Selva Biological Station, Palo Verde, and the Osa.

The platform's relationship with this community is curatorial, not gatekeeping. Conservation biologists are invited to review and endorse lesson content in their areas of expertise. An endorsement from a working researcher at UCR on the module about riparian buffer planting in lowland river systems — choosing the right native species for the soil type, spacing for flood resilience, the tradeoff between fast-establishing exotics and slower-growing natives — transforms that lesson from general guidance into locally calibrated best practice.

The feedback loop runs in both directions. Field data collected by student campaigns and elder-guided community monitoring flows back to the scientific advisors who helped design the lessons. A conservation biologist advising a module on pollinator corridor establishment in Guanacaste dry forest can, over two or three seasons, see whether the interventions students are logging are producing the species presence shifts the model predicts.

This is not a one-way knowledge transfer from expert to community. It is a genuine data partnership. The community provides what survey-based science rarely has: long-term presence, local continuity, and relationships with landowners that make access possible across seasons and years.

## What the platform looks like in practice

### A lesson flows from scientist to elder to student

A conservation biologist at CATIE advises the content for a module on native shade tree selection for smallholder coffee farms in the central valley — a practical, economically relevant lesson that sits at the intersection of agroforestry, carbon sequestration, and bird habitat. The module carries her name as scientific advisor.

A retired agroforester in Turrialba who has been managing shade coffee for thirty years reviews the module and contributes a supplementary practical note — what the module calls "community annotation" — about which species are actually obtainable from local nurseries versus which are theoretically correct but impossible to source. He earns a Knowledge Keeper credential for his contribution.

A biology teacher at a secondary school in Cartago builds a term project around the module. Her students visit a nearby farm, inventory the existing shade trees, assess the site against the lesson criteria, and propose a replanting schedule using the module's native species guide. They log their observations in the platform, linked to iNaturalist. They produce a field report that the elder-agroforester and the conservation biologist both review and endorse.

At the end of the term, each student has a verified portfolio entry — a field contribution they can describe concretely — and the school has contributed to a regional dataset on shade tree composition in smallholder coffee farms that did not exist before.

### An observation campaign runs across a watershed

A SINAC conservation area coordinator creates a campaign covering a 15-kilometre stream corridor that runs through three municipalities and past four schools. The target taxa are the native riparian trees listed under the corridor's restoration plan. The time window is the rainy season, when leaf flush makes identification easiest.

Four schools participate. Elders from two communities sign on as field guides — they know this stream; some of them planted trees here in the 1990s after a campaign that is now partially forgotten. Students work in pairs with a field guide. Observations go directly into the iNaturalist-linked platform. The coordinator sees the data aggregating in real time.

At the end of the campaign, the dataset shows which sections of the corridor have healthy canopy structure and which have gaps. The restoration plan for the following dry season is informed by this. The field report — student-authored, elder-reviewed, coordinator-verified — is submitted to SINAC as part of the watershed management record. The elder guides hold Biodiversity Observer credentials. The students hold field contribution certificates.

### A phenology network starts small and grows

A researcher at the Tropical Science Center in Monteverde is interested in the phenology of the resplendent quetzal's food trees — the wild avocado relatives that fruit at predictable times and around which quetzal movements organise each year. She designs a simple first-sighting protocol: log the date when you see a specific tree beginning to fruit.

The protocol goes into the platform as a seasonal observation campaign. Thirty participants sign up across the Monteverde cloud forest zone — students, guides, a retired park ranger, farmers on the forest edge. In the first year the data is sparse. In the third year, year-on-year comparison is automatic. In the fifth year, the dataset is long enough to see whether fruiting is shifting earlier, which is what the models predict under regional warming.

This is high scientific value generated from what is, for each individual participant, a five-minute weekly observation. The researcher gets a multi-site phenology dataset she could not have generated any other way. The participants get credentials, points, and the experience of contributing to something that will still be running in twenty years.

## The knowledge that is at risk

The urgency of this is not abstract. The generation of Costa Ricans who watched the great deforestation of the 1950s through the 1980s — who remember what the forests looked like before, who participated in the reforestation campaigns of the 1980s and 1990s, who know by direct experience which restoration methods worked and which did not — is in its seventies and eighties now.

Their knowledge is not in any database. It is not in the lesson library. It is not in the scientific literature, which mostly began arriving in the 1990s after much of the structural ecological knowledge had already been demonstrated by farmers and forest workers.

The window to record and transmit this knowledge is not wide. The platform described here is one mechanism for doing it — structured, recognised, and connected to the scientific and educational institutions that can amplify what the community already knows.

## Why Costa Rica first

Every component of this vision is more tractable in Costa Rica than almost anywhere else.

The legal framework for biodiversity conservation is strong and well-funded. SINAC manages eleven conservation areas with active scientific monitoring. Multiple universities run long-term ecological research programmes with data that can be shared with a community platform. The school system has an environmental education mandate. The ecotourism economy has created a culture in which biodiversity knowledge has visible economic value — guides, naturalists, farm tourism operators all have strong personal incentives to maintain and transmit ecological knowledge.

And Costa Rica has a conservation identity it defends. The country has staked national reputation on being the place that chose the forest over extraction. A platform that makes that commitment legible — that shows, school by school and stream corridor by stream corridor, what the community is actually doing and who knows what — is not a foreign import. It is an extension of something Costa Ricans have been building for seventy years.

## The invitation

We are not building this alone. The platform is infrastructure. What fills it is community knowledge, community data, and community decisions about what gets recognised and how.

If you are a conservation biologist working in Costa Rica whose research touches on the species or ecosystems described here, we want your input on the lesson library. If you are an educator coordinating field science programmes in Costa Rican schools, we want to design the student practicum integration with you. If you are someone who has spent decades learning what a specific forest or watershed actually does — in your hands, not just in your head — we want to give that knowledge a home it can keep.

The forest is old. The knowledge is older. The students are ready.
