# Five Places, Five Priorities

*Before Exotopia designs a single pollinator-stepping-stone planting plan for any of the regions in
our outreach pipeline, it's worth asking a more basic question first: what do the people who
actually live there say is urgent, right now? Importing one region's priority list into another —
assuming everyone is as focused on pollinator habitat as a Front Range gardener might be — is
exactly the mistake ["Designing a Municipal Compost Producer-Responsibility Campaign"](/blog/municipal-compost-campaign-strategy)
warns against on a smaller scale. This is that check, done properly, region by region.*

---

## Boulder & Douglas Counties, Colorado

<img src="/blog-assets/regional-maps/boulder-douglas-colorado.png" alt="Colorful OpenStreetMap-based map of Boulder and Douglas Counties, Colorado" style="width:100%;border-radius:8px;margin:12px 0" />

The dominant local concern right now isn't biodiversity in the abstract — it's **water and fire,
and they're the same story.** The City of Boulder entered Drought Watch on April 1, 2026, after a
snowpack that peaked at just 58% of normal and weeks earlier than usual; nearly the entire state is
in drought. About 88% of buildings in Boulder are rated high wildfire risk, and wildfire itself
threatens the water supply a second time over — burn scars trigger the flooding, erosion, and
sediment/metal runoff that fouls the same reservoirs the drought is already straining. Douglas
County, meanwhile, is actively working to diversify its water supply as population grows.

**Where a stepping-stone campaign actually fits here:** not as a competing priority, but riding
alongside the one that already has everyone's attention. Native, low-water pollinator plantings are
also defensible-space-appropriate and drought-tolerant landscaping — the same yard changes a
wildfire-risk-reduction conversation is already asking residents to make. A Colorado plan that
leads with "this is also good wildfire/water practice" will land better than one that leads with
pollinators alone.

## Costa Rica

<img src="/blog-assets/regional-maps/costa-rica.png" alt="Colorful OpenStreetMap-based map of Costa Rica" style="width:100%;border-radius:8px;margin:12px 0" />

Costa Rica's environmental reputation abroad (rainforest, ecotourism, "pura vida") sits alongside a
much less exported local reality: **pesticide exposure from the pineapple industry.** Costa Rica
uses one of the highest volumes of pesticides per hectare in the world, and communities near
pineapple plantations report elevated rates of illness linked to exposure — including chemicals
banned in the countries that import the fruit. Exports grew more than 200% between 2000 and 2013,
and the resulting deforestation, soil erosion, and 2009 wave of protests over pesticide runoff into
regional waterways are all part of the same story local communities live with directly. Even local
wildlife carries visible evidence: patches of yellow fur in howler monkeys living near pineapple and
banana plantations, linked to sulfur-heavy pesticide exposure.

**Where a stepping-stone campaign actually fits here:** less as a standalone pollinator-garden
program and more as part of a pesticide-reduction conversation already underway — a planting or
buffer-zone approach near affected communities and waterways that does double duty as habitat *and*
a literal barrier reducing pesticide drift, framed honestly as a smaller piece of a much bigger
producer-responsibility fight local advocates are already having with the export industry.

## Seoul, South Korea

<img src="/blog-assets/regional-maps/seoul-south-korea.png" alt="Colorful OpenStreetMap-based map of Seoul, South Korea" style="width:100%;border-radius:8px;margin:12px 0" />

Seoul's most immediate, visible environmental concern is **air quality** — the city ranked among
the world's ten most polluted major cities as recently as May 2026, driven by a mix of local
vehicle/industrial emissions and transboundary pollution. Layered on top of that is a fast-growing
**urban heat island** problem, intensified by the combination of climate change and dense
redevelopment — medium/high-density residential areas, industrial zones, and roads all measurably
raise local temperature, while greenery and taller, more porous urban form measurably cool it. The
city's own response leans directly on green infrastructure: a plan to grow green space citywide by
20% by 2030, plus a push for more green roofs specifically to fight the heat-island effect.

**Where a stepping-stone campaign actually fits here:** unusually well, actually — Seoul's own
official policy direction (more green space, more green roofs, explicitly for heat and air quality)
is close to a ready-made mandate a pollinator-corridor plan can plug directly into, rather than
having to make the case for green space from scratch. The framing that will land locally is cooling
and air quality first, pollinator habitat as the co-benefit — not the reverse.

## Lamu & Mpeketoni, Kenya

<img src="/blog-assets/regional-maps/lamu-mpeketoni-kenya.png" alt="Colorful OpenStreetMap-based map of the Lamu and Mpeketoni region, Kenya" style="width:100%;border-radius:8px;margin:12px 0" />

The defining local concern is **mangrove loss and what it takes with it.** Lamu county has lost
mangrove cover at roughly -0.26% per year, part of an estimated 15,587 degraded hectares across
Lamu and Tana per Kenya's National Mangrove Ecosystem Management Plan (2017–2027) — driven by
population growth, infrastructure development (notably the LAPSSET corridor), conversion for salt
extraction, oil exploration, and limited local livelihood alternatives. Mangroves aren't scenery
here — they're coastal protection, fisheries habitat, and carbon storage all at once, so their loss
is a direct livelihood threat to fishing communities, not an abstract ecological one. The
in-progress Lamu Port project adds a further concrete worry: dredging and construction effects on
the same mangroves and coral reefs local fishing depends on. Kenya's own 2026 policy response is
notable — a shift toward putting coastal communities at the *center* of mangrove conservation
rather than treating it as a top-down regulatory program.

**Where a stepping-stone campaign actually fits here:** the pollinator-stepping-stone frame
translates almost directly — mangrove replanting *is* stepping-stone habitat restoration, just for
a coastal/marine-adjacent guild rather than urban terrestrial pollinators, and the region already
has active, community-centered restoration infrastructure (per the PANORAMA-documented
community-based ecological mangrove restoration work) to plug into rather than duplicate.

## Nairobi, Kenya

<img src="/blog-assets/regional-maps/nairobi-kenya.png" alt="Colorful OpenStreetMap-based map of Nairobi, Kenya" style="width:100%;border-radius:8px;margin:12px 0" />

Nairobi's environmental burden falls hardest on its informal settlements. Around 60% of the city's
residents live in informal areas that make up only 5.2% of its residential land, and only about 40%
of Nairobi residents have sewage access at all — the Nairobi River system is consequently one of
the most polluted urban waterways in Africa, driving recurrent cholera and typhoid outbreaks. Air
pollution citywide runs several times WHO's recommended limit, worsened in informal settlements
specifically by unclean cooking fuel, waste burning, and small-scale polluting industry. At the same
time, Nairobi has something almost no other capital city does: **Nairobi National Park borders the
city directly** — visible on the map above as the large green area right at the city's southern
edge — meaning urban wildlife and habitat connectivity aren't a hypothetical here, they're a literal
fence line.

**Where a stepping-stone campaign actually fits here:** this is the region where biodiversity work
most needs to defer to more urgent priorities rather than lead — sanitation, water, and air quality
in informal settlements are not competing causes to a pollinator-planting program, they're the
higher-priority baseline that has to be acknowledged first. Where it genuinely fits is the
park-edge/green-corridor geography specifically: habitat connectivity work at the literal urban
edge of Nairobi National Park is a real, locally legible frame in a way a generic city-wide
pollinator campaign wouldn't be.

## What this means for how we sequence the work

Two of five regions (Seoul, Nairobi's park edge) have a biodiversity/green-space campaign that
plugs directly into an existing local priority. Two more (Boulder/Douglas, Lamu/Mpeketoni) have a
strong *adjacent* fit — wildfire-resilient landscaping, mangrove restoration — where the framing
just needs to lead with the locally-urgent concern rather than pollinators specifically. One
(Costa Rica) needs the work positioned as a small piece of a much larger, already-contentious
producer-responsibility fight, not a standalone garden program. None of the five are "just plant
pollinator gardens, done" — which is exactly why this snapshot came before any planting plan, not
after.
