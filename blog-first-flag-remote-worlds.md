# First Flag on a Remote World
## The preponderance of exoplanets in galaxy clusters — modelling, citizen science, and why a hypothesized world still deserves a name

**SCD Hub · Exotopia.org · Public draft · GPL v3**
*Working document — intended for release; verify all figures before publishing*

## The detection gap nobody talks about

Every exoplanet ever confirmed sits within roughly 8,500 light-years of Earth. The most sensitive instruments we have — Kepler, TESS, radial velocity spectrographs on 10-metre class telescopes — run out of precision far short of the nearest galaxy cluster.

The Virgo Cluster is 54 million light-years away. Coma is 330 million. Norma, which sits at the core of the Great Attractor and may contain 1,000 trillion stars across its member galaxies, is 220 million light-years from here.

We have confirmed zero exoplanets in any of these clusters. Not one.

This is not a scientific result. It is a detection limit. The physics of planetary transits, radial velocity wobble, and direct imaging all degrade catastrophically with distance. A planet the size of Jupiter transiting a Sun-like star in the Virgo Cluster produces a signal roughly 100 million times smaller than we can currently measure from that distance.

The absence of confirmed detections does not mean the absence of planets. It means we have not looked — because we cannot yet look. These are fundamentally different things, and conflating them is one of the more consequential confusions in how we talk about the universe.

## What the numbers actually say

In 2013, Erik Petigura and colleagues published an occurrence-rate analysis of the Kepler field that changed the conversation permanently. Approximately 22% of Sun-like stars host Earth-sized planets with orbital periods under 245 days. By 2021, Steve Bryson and the Kepler team had refined this: when you include the full habitable zone, roughly half of all Sun-like stars may harbour Earth-sized planets.

These are not estimates for our galactic neighbourhood. The Kepler sample is statistically representative of stellar populations throughout the Milky Way — and there is no physical mechanism that would make those occurrence rates different in another spiral galaxy, or in the elliptical and lenticular galaxies that dominate cluster cores. Stars form from the same physics. Protoplanetary discs obey the same equations. Metal-rich stars in the Virgo Cluster formed under conditions broadly similar to metal-rich stars here.

Let us run the numbers for the Virgo Cluster alone:

- **Confirmed member galaxies**: ~1,300 (VCC catalogue; true total likely 2,000+)
- **Stars per large elliptical**: 10¹¹ to 10¹³ (M87 alone contains an estimated 1 trillion)
- **Conservative total stellar count**: 10¹⁵ stars (1 quadrillion)
- **Occurrence rate at 10% (conservative)**: 10¹⁴ planets
- **Occurrence rate at 40% (Bryson et al.)**: 4 × 10¹⁴ planets

One hundred trillion to four hundred trillion planets. In a single galaxy cluster. Within a structure we can see from our back garden with a modest telescope on a clear night.

The Coma Cluster, at 330 million light-years, contains roughly twice as many galaxies and is far richer in massive ellipticals. Norma, sitting at the gravitational centre of the Great Attractor, draws dozens of surrounding clusters into its field. The Shapley Concentration — a supercluster spanning 200 million light-years — contains tens of thousands of galaxies.

The universe is not mostly empty. It is mostly worlds that we have not named.

## The exomoon dimension

The planet count is itself a floor, not a ceiling. Our own solar system contains 8 planets and more than 200 moons. The largest moons — Titan, Ganymede, Callisto — are geologically active worlds in their own right. Titan has an atmosphere thicker than Earth's, liquid methane lakes, and a hydrological cycle mediated by organic chemistry.

The first credible exomoon candidate, Kepler-1625b-i, was reported in 2018. The detection remains contested — the signal is at the edge of Kepler's sensitivity — but the statistical argument for exomoon ubiquity is solid. If gas giants form in most planetary systems (and the occurrence data suggests they do), and if moons form around gas giants by the same disc-fragmentation and capture processes that produced our outer solar system, then the number of detectable exomoon-hosting systems is likely within one or two orders of magnitude of the planet count.

This multiplies our Virgo estimate by somewhere between 10 and 100.

In unexplored regions — galaxy clusters, the space between filaments, the void-edge supercluster structures — we have no observational data at all on moons. We have strong theoretical priors. We have population synthesis models. We have the extrapolation of everything we know about planet and moon formation applied to stellar populations that are physically similar to our own.

What we do not have is any name attached to any of them.

## Why this matters for modelling and data management

The planetary population of a galaxy cluster is not an exotic philosophical question. It is a parameter that belongs in cluster models.

Modern N-body simulations of galaxy clusters track dark matter haloes, hot intracluster gas, stellar populations, supermassive black holes, and the dynamics of galaxy-galaxy and galaxy-ICM interactions. Planetary populations are currently absent from these models — not because they are dynamically irrelevant at cluster scales (they are mostly negligible), but because including them requires a data layer that does not exist.

Building that data layer is a citizen science problem.

When a field researcher in Lamu submits a water quality check-in linked to their exolocation deed on the Virgo Cluster, they are doing something unusual: they are connecting a real-world environmental measurement to a coordinate in a system where planetary populations are statistically certain but observationally uncharted. The record of that link — the timestamp, the GPS, the hash, the chain — is data. It is the beginning of a provenance trail that says: *someone was paying attention here, and they noted a connection between a place on Earth and a location in the cosmos, at this time.*

That is the raw material of citizen science. Not polished. Not peer-reviewed. But timestamped, tamper-evident, and permanent.

As the survey data accumulates — more exolocation deeds, more eco-ops check-ins, more Water Quality Certificates anchored to specific cluster coordinates — patterns may emerge. Which regions of the cluster are most represented? Which settlement owners are most active? Where does the community imagine planets, and why?

None of this replaces professional astronomy. All of it supplements it in ways that professional astronomy is not currently doing.

## The upgrade chain as scientific method

The Exotopia NFT tier system was designed for a specific practical purpose: to handle the evolving certainty of exoplanet detection across four tiers.

| Tier | Status | Evidence required | Rarity |
|---|---|---|---|
| THEORETICAL | Statistically expected, location unspecified | Population synthesis | 10× |
| FRONTIER | Specific star known, planet statistically predicted | Hipparcos/Gaia parallax + occurrence rate | 5× |
| CANDIDATE | Transit or RV signal detected | TOI / KOI designation | 2× |
| CONFIRMED | NASA archive, peer-reviewed | Published discovery | 1× |

This is the scientific method encoded as a token upgrade chain.

A theoretical deed in the Virgo Cluster makes a falsifiable claim: *there is almost certainly a planet in this region of this cluster.* That claim cannot be verified with current instruments. It may not be verifiable within the lifetime of anyone alive today. But it can be falsified — if we one day survey that region with sufficient precision and find no planets — and it can be confirmed, if we detect one.

The philosopher Karl Popper argued that the mark of a scientific claim is its capacity to be falsified. A theoretical exolocation deed is falsifiable. It is, in that specific sense, a scientific claim.

The deed is also timestamped. The blockchain record shows when the claim was made, who made it, and what coordinate system was used. If a planet is confirmed in that region in 2085, the record of who hypothesized it first is already on-chain, immutable, and public.

This is the digital equivalent of leaving a flag.

## The first flag

When the Apollo 11 crew planted the American flag in the Sea of Tranquility in July 1969, the flag was a symbol rather than a legal claim — the Outer Space Treaty of 1967 had already established that no nation could claim sovereignty over the Moon. But the *act* of planting it was data. It said: humans were here, at this coordinate, at this time. That fact is permanent.

Exotopia theoretical deeds work the same way. They are not property claims in any legal sense. The exolocation deed for a position in the Virgo Cluster does not confer ownership of a galaxy, or a star, or a hypothetical planet orbiting that star. What it does is create a permanent record: *someone was paying attention to this coordinate, at this time, and they noted that a planet here is statistically highly likely.*

The record includes the person's wallet address, their community affiliation, their eco-ops history, their mule-bot's knowledge of local land and water conditions. The virtual outpost at that cluster coordinate is connected to a real person doing real field work on a real planet — this one — in real time.

That connection is what makes the deed meaningful. It is not a speculative investment. It is a statement of relationship: between a field researcher in Kilifi and a star 54 million light-years away that almost certainly has worlds of its own that have never been named.

## Outpost exomoons and the unexplored frontier

The planetary case for galaxy clusters extends to their moons. If a gas giant in the Virgo Cluster exists — and the occurrence data says it almost certainly does — then that gas giant almost certainly has moons. Some of those moons may be the size of Mars or larger. Some may be geologically active, heated by tidal forces from their host planet. Some may be within the liquid-water habitable zone of their parent star.

These objects have never appeared in any catalogue. They have never been named. They have never been argued about, or theorised over, or pointed at by a child through a telescope in a dark field outside a city.

They are simply out there, unnamed, in structures that have names — Virgo, Coma, Norma, the Shapley Concentration — but whose individual worlds remain entirely outside human record.

The argument for hypothesizing them — for placing a theoretical marker and a name at a statistical location within a cluster — is the same argument that motivated every naming convention humans have ever developed for the sky: because naming is attention, and attention is the beginning of knowledge.

A Theoretical NFT in the Coma Cluster acknowledges that something is there. It is a placeholder for knowledge that does not exist yet. It is a question posed in a form that will outlast the person who asked it.

## What citizen science can do here

Professional astronomy will not survey the exoplanet populations of galaxy clusters within any near-term timeframe. The instruments do not exist. The funding is allocated elsewhere. The detection methods scale badly with distance.

Citizen science can contribute at the edges of this problem in at least four ways:

**1. Catalogue maintenance and gap analysis.** The VCC (Virgo Cluster Catalogue) and FCC (Fornax Cluster Catalogue) are well-studied but incomplete. Community members can cross-reference public data (NED, SIMBAD, Vizier) against the Exotopia cluster member files and flag where data is thin, contradictory, or missing. This kind of structured gap analysis — done carefully and documented — is a genuine contribution to data management even without access to a telescope.

**2. Occurrence rate modelling.** Applying published occurrence rates (Petigura, Bryson, Fressin) to specific cluster member populations — accounting for host star type, metallicity, cluster age — produces probability distributions for planetary populations per cluster. This is undergraduate-level statistics applied to public data. A well-documented community model is citable.

**3. Amateur transit monitoring.** For the very nearest clusters (Virgo at 16.5 Mpc is still ~1,000× too far for current instruments, but Proxima Centauri-scale future targets may bring cluster-peripheral surveys within reach in decades), amateur photometry networks can contribute timing data and baseline luminosity records that professional surveys use for target selection.

**4. Theoretical deed records as a longitudinal dataset.** If thousands of people place theoretical exolocation deeds in specific galaxy clusters over the next decade, and if those deeds are associated with real-world eco-ops activity, the aggregate dataset becomes something unusual: a human-interest record of which parts of the distant universe people found worth hypothesizing about, correlated with their real-world environmental activity. That dataset has value — not as astronomy, but as the sociology and psychology of how communities relate to the cosmos. It is the kind of data that gets cited in unexpected places.

## The Exotopia position

Exotopia theoretical deeds are explicitly labelled as theoretical. The NFT metadata includes the tier, the evidence basis, and the confidence level. No deed claims certainty it does not have.

What a theoretical deed in the Virgo Cluster does claim is this: *given what we know about stellar populations and planetary occurrence rates, a planet within this cluster coordinate is statistically nearly certain to exist.* That claim is honest. It is grounded in peer-reviewed science. And it connects a real person — with a name, a community, a field practice, a mule-bot — to a remote region of the universe that has never before had any human connection.

The flag is theoretical. The planting of it is real.

## Open questions — for further research and community discussion

1. **What is the minimum cluster metallicity at which terrestrial planet occurrence rates hold?** High-metallicity stars form planets more reliably. Cluster metallicity distributions vary. The theoretical population for a metal-poor cluster like some Virgo outliers may be significantly lower than the headline 40%.

2. **Tidal disruption in cluster cores: does the BCG environment suppress planetary formation?** The intracluster medium and strong tidal fields near BCG galaxies (M87 in Virgo, NGC4889 in Coma) may affect circumstellar disc lifetimes. Are planetary populations depleted near BCGs?

3. **Ram-pressure stripping and planet survival.** Galaxies falling into clusters have their gas stripped by the intracluster medium. Does this affect stellar nurseries? Does it affect existing planetary systems? There is some evidence that ram-pressure stripping can trigger star formation rather than suppressing it.

4. **Exomoon habitability constraints in cluster environments.** The X-ray luminosity of the intracluster medium is significant. What flux does the ICM deliver to moons in the outer reaches of cluster member galaxies? Is it a radiation hazard, or negligible at planetary distances from the host star?

5. **What does a first confirmed exoplanet in a galaxy cluster look like?** What instrument, what method, what target? Is microlensing a viable route? Could JWST-era successor telescopes detect atmospheric features of a transiting planet in M87 — or is that physics permanently out of reach?
