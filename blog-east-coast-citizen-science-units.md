# Ticks, Toxic Blooms, and the Pulse of Freshwater: Three Citizen Science Programs for East Coast Participants

## A call for subject matter experts, field trainers, and community participants — and an invitation to help build the curriculum

**SCD Hub / Exotopia.org — June 2026**

---

The East Coast of the United States is experiencing three converging ecological pressures that citizen science can directly address. All three generate data that matters to public health, watershed management, and climate adaptation. All three can be investigated with tools that cost under $50 and skills that can be taught in a half-day. And all three have significant gaps in community-level knowledge, practitioner training capacity, and longitudinal data collection that a distributed citizen science network is uniquely positioned to fill.

This post introduces three SCD Hub citizen science curriculum units — two East Coast-specific, one national — outlines the educational framework for each, and issues a direct call for subject matter experts (SMEs), field trainers, K–12 and community college educators, and participants to help build and run them.

## Program 1: Tick Watch Northeast

### Why this, why now

Blacklegged tick (*Ixodes scapularis*) populations have expanded north and inland across the Northeast at an accelerating pace — a well-documented consequence of milder winters, higher deer densities, and forest fragmentation. Lyme disease (caused by *Borrelia burgdorferi*) is now the most common vector-borne disease in the United States. But Lyme is no longer the only concern: anaplasmosis, babesiosis, Powassan virus, and ehrlichiosis are all tick-borne and all increasing in incidence along the Atlantic seaboard.

The surveillance gap is structural. State tick surveillance programs operate on limited staffing and sporadic sampling schedules. Academic drag-sampling networks exist at several universities but are geographically sparse. iNaturalist tick observations are not standardised for epidemiological use. A coordinated, protocol-consistent citizen science network could produce the kind of geographic and seasonal granularity that public health officials currently cannot access.

### Life cycle overview (educational unit anchor)

The blacklegged tick has a two-year, three-host life cycle:

1. **Larva** (August–September): hatches from egg, feeds once on a small mammal host (white-footed mouse is the primary Lyme reservoir), drops off and overwinters
2. **Nymph** (May–July): most epidemiologically dangerous stage — small enough to go unnoticed, often already infected from larval blood meal, actively questing in leaf litter and shrubby edges through early summer
3. **Adult** (October–November, and brief early spring window): larger, more visible; females quest on taller vegetation; seek large mammal hosts (deer, humans, dogs)

Understanding the three-stage cycle is the key to understanding when and where risk is highest — the nymph peak in May through July, not high summer — and it is the anchor concept for the educational unit.

### Project-based learning unit: Tick Index Mapping

**Target audience:** Secondary school (grades 7–12), community groups, Master Gardener volunteers, trail maintenance crews  
**Duration:** 4–8 sessions (can be implemented as a semester-length longitudinal project or a two-day intensive)

**Core activities:**

- **Habitat transect assessment** — participants map school grounds, parks, or trail corridors for tick risk factors: leaf litter depth, shrub density, presence of deer trails, proximity of forest-lawn ecotone (the primary risk zone). Recorded on platform site map.
- **Drag-cloth sampling** — standardised 1m × 1m white flannel cloth dragged 10m transects through identified habitat. Tick counts per transect recorded by life stage and species. Method adapted from TickSpotters/URI protocol.
- **Submission and identification** — ticks photographed on white background (10cm scale reference), submitted to TickSpotters (University of Rhode Island) for species ID and estimated engorgement stage. Results auto-link to platform observation record.
- **Personal protection practicum** — hands-on application of DEET (children's formulation), permethrin-treated clothing demonstration, tick removal technique (fine-tipped tweezers, straight pull — no twisting, no petroleum jelly), 72-hour monitoring for expanding rash (erythema migrans diagnostic indicator)
- **Longitudinal record** — groups tracking the same sites across seasons contribute to a multi-year tick index. This is the data that is currently missing from the public health record.

**Learning outcomes:** Students and participants can explain the tick life cycle and peak risk periods, identify a blacklegged tick nymph versus an adult, perform a proper body check and tick removal, locate their county's tick surveillance report, and interpret drag-sampling results.

**Platform certificate:** `eco:citizen-science — Tick Index Mapping, Northeast` — co-signable by school, public health department, or state university extension office

## Tick ecology deep dive: control methods, their costs, and who else lives in the system

*This section is part of the Tick Watch Northeast curriculum library — intended for educators, land managers, and participants who want to understand the full ecological picture behind tick control decisions. Peer review welcome: contact acmeideal@gmail.com.*

---

### The standard chemical toolkit: pyrethrum, pyrethroids, and tick tubes

When land managers, homeowners, or public health programmes reach for chemical tick control, they are almost always reaching for one of two classes of product: natural pyrethrum or synthetic pyrethroids.

**Pyrethrum** is extracted from the dried flowers of *Tanacetum cinerariifolium* (sometimes called the Dalmatian chrysanthemum). Its active compounds — pyrethrin I and pyrethrin II — disrupt sodium channels in insect nervous systems, causing rapid knockdown. Pyrethrum degrades quickly in sunlight and water, which is ecologically significant: its residual toxicity in the environment is low. Commercial formulations often add piperonyl butoxide (PBO) as a synergist, which inhibits insect detoxification enzymes and increases potency but also extends environmental persistence somewhat and has its own toxicity profile.

**Pyrethroids** are synthetic compounds that mimic and amplify the pyrethrin mechanism while dramatically extending environmental persistence. Permethrin, bifenthrin, deltamethrin, and lambda-cyhalothrin are the ones most commonly applied in residential and commercial tick management. Of these, permethrin is by far the most widely used in consumer products. Bifenthrin is common in professional landscape applications and is significantly more persistent in soil and sediment than permethrin.

Both natural pyrethrum and synthetic pyrethroids are used in two primary application modes for tick control:

**Mode 1 — Broadcast acaricide spraying.** Vegetation and leaf litter at the forest-lawn ecotone is treated by licensed applicators (or homeowners with consumer products) on a scheduled basis — typically at nymph peak (May–June) and adult peak (October). The goal is to reduce tick populations at the interface zone where human exposure is highest.

**Mode 2 — Tick tubes (Damminix-type products).** Cardboard tubes — visually similar to toilet paper rolls — are stuffed with permethrin-treated cotton balls and placed in areas actively used by white-footed mice: near stone walls, brush piles, wood stacks, dense ground cover. Mice collect the cotton for nesting material. Nymphal ticks that would have fed on the mouse encounter the permethrin in the nest and die before they can transmit pathogens. The method is elegantly targeted: it attacks the tick-mouse interface, the precise moment in the life cycle where *Borrelia burgdorferi* (Lyme disease) is acquired by larval ticks from the animal that carries it best.

Field studies on tick tube programmes have reported 68–97% reduction in nymphal tick density in treated areas under optimal conditions. However, effectiveness drops significantly when coverage is incomplete (mice commute across untreated boundaries), when mouse populations are high (diluting the per-mouse treatment effect), and when application timing misses the larval intake window in late summer.

### Biodiversity impacts: what else do these products affect?

#### Aquatic invertebrates

This is the most serious ecological concern and the one most directly relevant to Tick Watch Northeast participants who are also monitoring macroinvertebrates.

Pyrethroids — permethrin and bifenthrin in particular — are among the most acutely toxic chemicals to aquatic invertebrates in common use. The aquatic toxicity of bifenthrin for sensitive species (stonefly nymphs, mayfly nymphs, midges) is measured in nanograms per litre: concentrations far below what human senses can detect are enough to cause acute mortality or sub-lethal behavioural disruption. These are, of course, the same organisms that the Freshwater Phenology Network uses as water quality bioindicators. A stream BMWP score can drop significantly after a single spray event if product reaches the water via surface runoff or spray drift.

**Pyrethrum (natural pyrethrins) is less persistent but similarly acutely toxic to aquatic invertebrates.** The reassurance that "it breaks down quickly" is true in full sunlight — but in shaded stream channels, under canopy cover, in leaf litter, pyrethrin degradation is much slower. "Breaks down in sunlight" does not mean "safe near water."

Tick tube cotton, once placed in mouse nests underground or under debris, is much less likely to reach waterways than broadcast spray. The aquatic risk from tick tubes is primarily indirect (via treated nesting material that decomposes over time) and generally considered substantially lower than from broadcast application. This is a meaningful ecological advantage of the targeted method.

**Regulatory buffer zones:** Most state regulations require a 100-foot setback from water bodies for pesticide application in residential settings. In practice, enforcement is low, and many homeowners are unaware of the requirement. Participants monitoring streams near residential areas should note whether spray application is occurring within buffer zones — this is observable and reportable data.

#### Pollinators and beneficial arthropods

Pyrethroids are broadly toxic to bees and other pollinators, though the practical risk from tick control spraying is lower than from agricultural application because residential spraying is typically done in early morning before foraging begins, and residue on foliage degrades within days. The risk is highest during spring wildflower bloom (April–May), which coincides with the nymph peak timing when spraying is most common. Operators are supposed to avoid spraying flowering plants; they frequently do not.

Spiders, ground beetles (Carabidae), and predatory mites — all of which consume ticks — are highly sensitive to pyrethroids. Broad-spectrum spraying in the leaf litter zone eliminates the natural arthropod predator community that provides some buffering of tick populations. There is a direct tension here: spraying to reduce ticks also reduces the tick's predators, potentially requiring more frequent future treatment.

#### Songbirds

Ground-feeding songbirds (thrushes, towhees, ovenbirds) forage in leaf litter in exactly the zones that receive treatment. There is documented evidence of sub-lethal exposure through invertebrate prey items in treated areas. Short-term reductions in arthropod prey after treatment may contribute to reduced reproductive success. The long-term population-level impact of residential tick spraying on forest-interior songbirds is an active area of concern among wildlife ecologists and is under-studied at the community level.

#### The tick tube exception

Of the chemical methods described, tick tubes have the narrowest off-target footprint. The primary non-target concern is cavity-nesting birds that might pull treated cotton from tubes near nest boxes (rare, as commercial tubes are designed to limit access by anything larger than a mouse), and small mammals other than *Peromyscus leucopus* (white-footed mouse) that may also collect the cotton — chipmunks, voles, meadow jumping mice. These are secondary reservoir hosts for some tick-borne pathogens, so their exposure is a mixed-effect: potentially reducing ticks on those hosts, with some mortality risk.

### Tick population impact: does it work?

The honest answer is: yes, locally and temporarily, with important caveats.

**Broadcast spraying** produces measurable short-term reductions in tick density in treated zones — studies consistently show 68–90%+ efficacy immediately after treatment. Recolonisation from adjacent untreated areas occurs within weeks to months. There is no evidence that annual spraying programmes permanently suppress tick populations at the landscape scale; ticks in surrounding forest and untreated properties continue reproducing. Annual treatment creates a cycle of dependence rather than a long-term solution.

**Tick tubes** target a specific bottleneck (the larval blood meal on mice) that is genuinely important in the transmission pathway. They do not kill adult ticks, do not affect ticks questing on deer, and have no impact on the winter tick (*Dermacentor albipictus*) because *Dermacentor* uses large mammals, not mice, as hosts. Their effect is also limited to the year following deployment — larvae that do not feed on treated mice this fall won't become infected nymphs next spring.

Neither method addresses the white-tailed deer population, which is the primary reproductive host for adult blacklegged ticks. As long as deer densities remain high, adult ticks will complete their reproductive cycle regardless of what happens to nymphs and larvae. Reducing deer density — through sustained hunting in suburban contexts — has stronger evidence behind it as a long-term tick population reduction strategy than any chemical treatment at the landscape scale, but is logistically and politically complex.

### Watershed impact: what happens when it rains?

Tick control products applied within residential watersheds enter the stream network through several pathways:

- **Surface runoff** during and after treatment events, particularly on slopes and impervious surfaces
- **Subsurface lateral flow** through shallow organic soil layers (relevant in forested settings with high organic matter)
- **Spray drift** during application — particularly when sprayers operate near stream buffers
- **Treated clipping and debris** entering drainage during mowing after treatment

The chemical most studied in this context is bifenthrin, which binds strongly to organic matter and persists in sediment for weeks to months. Urban stormwater runoff surveys have detected bifenthrin in stream sediments at concentrations that are acutely toxic to sensitive macroinvertebrates — consistently, across multiple states, including in streams not near obvious point sources. Permethrin is less persistent but more water-soluble and more likely to be transported in the dissolved phase.

**The BMWP connection:** If your class or community group is maintaining a stream BMWP baseline, your monitoring data before and after local spray seasons can directly document this effect. A drop in Ephemeroptera (mayflies), Plecoptera (stoneflies), or Trichoptera (caddisflies) — the EPT score — in a stream adjacent to a treated watershed is a data point. It is not proof of causation on its own, but combined with treatment timing records and weather data, it becomes part of the evidence that regulators and watershed planners need. This is exactly the kind of longitudinal citizen data that is currently absent from the watershed management record.

**Rain gardens as buffers:** The rain garden intervention described in the Cyanobacteria Watch unit applies here too. A well-designed rain garden with deep-rooted native plantings intercepts stormwater sheet flow from treated turf and filters it through soil before it reaches a stream. This does not eliminate pyrethroid transport but substantially reduces runoff velocity and volume. Sites where rain gardens are being installed should be considered for paired upstream/downstream BMWP monitoring to document pre/post effects.

### Human and pet health: what participants should know

**For adults:** Permethrin as used in tick tube cotton and permethrin-treated clothing is poorly absorbed through intact skin and is rapidly metabolised by most mammals. The permethrin-treated clothing standard (factory-treated gear at 0.5% permethrin, rated to 70+ washes) has a strong safety record and is among the most effective personal tick protection tools available. Field application of permethrin to clothing (spray products) requires care to allow full drying before wearing — wet permethrin against skin is more concerning than dried permethrin.

**For cats: this is a serious safety issue.** Cats lack a key hepatic enzyme (glucuronyl transferase) that most mammals use to detoxify pyrethroids. Permethrin exposure that would cause at most mild symptoms in a dog or human — concentrated spot-on flea treatments, deliberate application, or even significant contact with freshly treated vegetation — can be acutely fatal in cats. Participants applying permethrin to clothing or treating outdoor areas should be aware that cats should not have contact with treated surfaces until fully dry, and that concentrated professional-grade products should never be applied to or near cats.

**For children:** Emerging research suggests cumulative organophosphate and pyrethroid exposure during early childhood may be associated with neurodevelopmental effects. The evidence is not settled and effect sizes are debated, but the precautionary principle suggests minimising residential spraying in areas where young children play, and using tick tubes and personal protection as the primary control strategy where possible.

**For applicators:** Licensed professional applicators are at higher chronic exposure risk than homeowners making seasonal applications. Proper PPE (nitrile gloves, face shield) during mixing and loading is important; the concentrate formulations used by professionals are orders of magnitude more concentrated than consumer products.

**Pyrethrum (natural) allergy:** A small percentage of people have clinically significant contact allergy to pyrethrum extract. The relevant population is those with known chrysanthemum/daisy allergies. Natural pyrethrum is not inherently "safe" for all people by virtue of being plant-derived.

### Alternative and complementary approaches

The most ecologically sound tick management strategy for a residential or community site combines several approaches, none of which alone is as effective as the combined effect:

**1. Japanese barberry removal.** *Berberis thunbergii*, the invasive ornamental shrub that covers the understorey of much of the Northeast's suburban woodlands, creates a humidity microclimate at ground level that is significantly more favourable to tick survival than the surrounding habitat. Studies in Connecticut and New York have found that tick densities inside barberry thickets are substantially higher than in comparable areas without barberry. Removing barberry — ideally combined with native shrub restoration — is one of the most ecologically effective site-level interventions. It also benefits native plant communities, bird habitat, and understorey biodiversity. It is slow, unglamorous work, and it is underused.

**2. Leaf litter management at the ecotone.** The forest-lawn edge is the highest-risk zone for tick encounter. A 3-foot width of wood chips or gravel between the mowed lawn and the forest edge creates a desiccating barrier that substantially reduces the number of ticks crossing into the human-use zone. Ticks are susceptible to desiccation — they need high humidity to survive questing. A dry barrier exploits this vulnerability.

**3. Entomopathogenic fungi.** *Metarhizium brunneum* (registered as Met52 or BioAct WP in the US) is a soil-dwelling fungus that parasitises arthropods. It is EPA-registered for tick control and has shown 60–90%+ reduction in tick density in field trials in forested settings. It does not persist indefinitely in the environment, but its non-target profile is substantially better than chemical acaricides: it is benign to mammals, fish, and most non-target arthropods at application rates tested. It is more expensive than permethrin per application and less widely available. This is one of the most promising alternatives for sites where aquatic impact is a concern.

**4. Encouraging opossums.** The Virginia opossum (*Didelphis virginiana*) is one of the most effective tick control agents in the Northeast, and it is underappreciated to the point of being actively discouraged. Opossums groom themselves obsessively, and ticks that attempt to feed on an opossum have approximately a 5% survival rate. A foraging opossum kills in the range of 4,000–5,000 ticks per season through grooming alone. Opossums are also immune to rabies (their body temperature is too low for the virus to survive) and are dead-end hosts for *Borrelia burgdorferi*. Native to the Northeast and expanding their range northward, they are frequently the victim of road mortality and negative perception. Educating communities about opossum ecology and discouraging harassment or vehicle-sourced mortality is a tick control intervention that costs nothing.

**5. Guinea fowl and wild turkeys.** Guinea fowl (a domestic bird) actively hunt insects including ticks in vegetation and are widely used on small farm properties for this purpose. Wild turkeys scratch in leaf litter and consume tick larvae along with other invertebrates. Neither is practical for suburban residential settings, but both are appropriate additions to farm-scale or community-property tick management planning.

**6. Deer exclusion fencing.** An 8-foot deer exclusion fence (or a double fence of two 4-foot fences separated by several feet) reliably excludes deer. In areas where adult tick populations are driven by deer access, exclusion fencing combined with other measures produces the best documented long-term results. The barrier is expensive to install and maintain and is impractical at landscape scale, but is appropriate for schoolyards, community gardens, and properties where children have regular outdoor access.

**7. Targeted 4-poster deer treatment stations.** 4-poster devices dispense acaricide to the ears and neck of deer as they feed at a corn station. Studies in Maryland, Virginia, and New York have found substantial local reductions in adult tick density where 4-poster networks achieve adequate coverage density. They require permits in some states and sustained maintenance. They are more ecologically targeted than broadcast spraying but still apply a chemical acaricide to wildlife.

### Wildlife ecology: moose, deer, mice, and opossums in the tick system

Understanding tick control without understanding tick hosts is like trying to manage a watershed without a map of where the water comes from. The tick life cycle involves a cast of wildlife species with very different roles.

#### White-footed mouse: the infection engine

*Peromyscus leucopus* is the primary reservoir host for *Borrelia burgdorferi* — the bacterium that causes Lyme disease — and for several co-infections including *Anaplasma phagocytophilum* (anaplasmosis) and *Babesia microti* (babesiosis). When a larval blacklegged tick feeds on an infected white-footed mouse in late summer, the tick acquires the pathogen. That tick overwinters, moults to nymph the following spring, and quests as an infected nymph through May–July — the period of highest human risk.

The white-footed mouse thrives in fragmented forest, exactly the habitat type that dominates the suburban Northeast. Mouse densities are high where predator communities (fox, coyote, owl, hawk) are suppressed by suburban development. High mouse density → high infection prevalence in the nymph population → high human disease risk.

Tick tubes target this bottleneck precisely. Their ecological logic is sound. Their limitation is scale.

#### White-tailed deer: the reproductive platform

White-tailed deer (*Odocoileus virginianus*) do not amplify Lyme disease — a deer that carries *B. burgdorferi* in its blood does not infect a feeding tick. Deer are ecologically important in the tick system for a different reason: adult female blacklegged ticks must take a blood meal from a large mammal to reproduce. A single engorged female tick on a deer produces approximately 2,000 eggs. Deer are the reproductive platform of the entire tick population. Without deer, tick populations collapse regardless of the infection reservoir.

Deer densities in the suburban Northeast — where hunting pressure is low, predators are absent, and forest fragmentation provides abundant edge habitat — are dramatically above historical norms. This is the root driver of the tick population expansion. Chemical control methods applied at the residential scale are treating a symptom of a regional ecological problem.

#### Moose: the winter tick tragedy

The moose (*Alces alces*) presents a different and more acute crisis.

The winter tick (*Dermacentor albipictus*) is a one-host tick: larvae, nymphs, and adults all complete their development on the same individual moose. Unlike the blacklegged tick with its complex three-host cycle, the winter tick attaches as a larva in fall, overwinters on the moose, moults through its life stages while feeding continuously, and drops off in spring as an engorged adult female ready to lay eggs. A single moose may carry between 47,000 and 90,000 winter ticks. Documented cases have exceeded 100,000 ticks on a single animal.

The physiological consequence is catastrophic. Ticks extract blood — a moose carrying 90,000 ticks is losing a significant fraction of its blood volume daily. The host responds with intense grooming: scratching, rubbing against trees, biting at its own flanks. The grooming breaks off the hollow guard hairs that insulate the animal, leaving bare, white skin exposed. The result is "ghost moose" — animals that appear almost white or grey in spring, stripped of their dark winter coat. Ghost moose are simultaneously anaemic and hypothermic. Calf mortality is the primary impact. In years of high winter tick burden, moose calf survival in New Hampshire and Maine has been documented at disturbingly low rates.

**Climate change is driving the crisis.** Winter ticks drop from the moose in spring ready to lay eggs. Larval ticks must then find a host in fall or they die. Snowfall timing is critical: larvae emerge and quest for moose in October and November. If snow covers the ground early, larvae that fail to find a host die of desiccation when they cannot move through snow to quest. If snowpack is late — as warming climate increasingly produces — more larvae survive to find moose and more moose carry heavier burdens. The northern range limit of the moose-winter tick system is shifting, with moose populations in southern portions of their range (Vermont, New Hampshire, Maine south of the White Mountains) under the greatest stress.

**The Lyme disease connection is indirect.** Moose do carry blacklegged ticks as well as winter ticks, but they are dead-end or low-competency hosts for *B. burgdorferi* — moose blood is inhospitable to the spirochete in ways deer blood is not. The moose-winter tick crisis is ecologically distinct from the moose-Lyme disease discussion, though both involve tick burden on the same species.

**Is direct tick removal from moose a viable intervention?** At the individual level, it is practiced in wildlife rehabilitation settings and in research protocols where animals are tranquilized. Researchers have documented that removing ticks from individual moose in managed contexts substantially reduces winter mortality. At population scale, it is not feasible — the animals are wild, the habitat is remote, and the numbers are too large. The practical interventions being studied are:

- Mineral lick stations modified to deliver acaricide to moose as they feed (adapted from the deer 4-poster concept)
- Targeted fly spraying at calving grounds during the attachment window (highly controversial)
- Habitat management to create refugia where tick larvae cannot survive (open, sunlit areas where larvae desiccate)
- Predator restoration (wolves and mountain lions regulate deer populations; deer reduction reduces all tick burdens)
- Snowpack forecasting and adaptive management (planning moose hunting quotas and supplemental interventions based on predicted tick burden for a given year based on fall weather)

#### Chipmunks, shrews, and squirrels: secondary reservoirs

Eastern chipmunks (*Tamias striatus*) are secondary reservoirs for *B. burgdorferi* and amplify infection in the nymphal tick population. Short-tailed shrews (*Blarina brevicauda*) are also competent reservoirs and are consumed by ticks at high rates. Gray squirrels are less competent than mice or chipmunks but widespread and abundant. The "dilution effect" hypothesis — that high biodiversity reduces tick-borne disease risk by increasing the proportion of ticks feeding on non-competent hosts — has been debated in tick ecology for twenty years. The current evidence suggests host diversity does not reliably dilute Lyme disease risk in northeastern communities; the reservoir-competent small mammal community is too abundant and too species-rich.

### Citizen data science: a Kaggle challenge for moose tick burden mapping

One of the structural problems in moose-winter tick research is that the most important data — tick burden by location and season — is expensive to collect. Tranquilizing moose for tick counts is resource-intensive; trail cameras capture some behavioural signal but require trained observers to interpret; road-killed moose provide opportunistic samples but with obvious survivorship bias.

A distributed citizen science network combined with spatial data science tools offers a different approach. The following is a proposed challenge structure intended for submission to Kaggle as a public dataset and competition:

---

**Proposed challenge: Northeast Moose Tick Burden — Spatial Prediction from Citizen Observations**

**Data inputs (assembled from public sources and citizen contributions):**

| Layer | Source |
|---|---|
| Moose sighting records with behaviour annotations | iNaturalist (filtered) + SCD Hub platform submissions |
| Trail camera observations with condition scores | Citizen uploads + NH Fish & Game, Maine IF&W open data |
| Snowpack depth and date-of-first-snow | NOAA, NRCS SNOTEL |
| Fall temperature anomaly (Oct–Nov) | PRISM / ERA5 |
| Forest type and canopy cover | NLCD, US Forest Service |
| Forest patch size and edge density | Derived from NLCD |
| Deer density estimates | State wildlife agency open data |
| Winter tick adult drop timing | Research literature range estimates |
| Road mortality data (moose) | State DOT records |
| Human land cover within 5km buffer | NLCD |

**Citizen science contribution protocol:**

Anyone with access to moose habitat can contribute:

1. **Moose sighting with body condition score.** On a simple 3-point scale: (1) normal appearance, dark coat, alert; (2) patchy coat, visible hair loss on flanks or neck, frequent scratching or rubbing; (3) severe hair loss ("ghost" condition), visible skin, reduced body condition. Submitted via iNaturalist tick observation project or SCD Hub platform with GPS point and date. A reference photo guide (adapted from University of New Hampshire wildlife lab materials) is provided with the protocol.

2. **Tick drag sampling in moose habitat.** Standard protocol adapted for forest settings: 100m transect with 1m² drag cloth, recording nymphal and larval counts by 10m segment. Moose hair and scat presence noted within 20m of transect. Submitted to the platform with coordinates and canopy condition data.

3. **Trail camera station deployment.** Participants with permission to deploy cameras on public or private forest land submit camera placement coordinates and monthly observation counts with behaviour annotation (grooming frequency, body condition). A standardised camera deployment protocol is provided.

4. **Snow date and depth records.** First snowfall date and depth at monitoring site, recorded through the winter. These are among the most predictive inputs for tick burden modelling but are not available at fine enough spatial resolution from NOAA alone.

**The Kaggle competition structure:**

The assembled dataset — citizen observations, climate inputs, wildlife agency data, land cover — is published as a public Kaggle dataset. The competition proposes two tracks:

- **Track 1 (prediction):** Predict moose body condition score (proxy for tick burden) at unseen observation locations given the climate, land cover, deer density, and forest patch features for that point. Evaluation by macro-F1 score.

- **Track 2 (spatial analysis):** Identify high-risk landscape configurations — the combination of forest type, patch size, deer density, and snowpack pattern that produces the highest probability of severe tick burden. Interpretable models (not just black-box predictions) are weighted in the scoring rubric, as the goal is to produce findings that wildlife managers can act on.

**Why Kaggle specifically:** Kaggle's public dataset infrastructure allows citizen contributors to add observation CSV files directly to the dataset repository. The leaderboard format attracts the data science community. Winning models and their feature importance outputs are public. The competition outputs a spatial prediction layer — a map showing predicted tick burden risk across the Northeast by season — that can be published through the SCD Hub platform and contributed to state wildlife agency planning processes.

**Connection to the bounty system:** SCD Hub bounties will be available for qualifying moose observations submitted via the protocol: body condition assessments with GPS and photo, and tick drag transects in moose habitat. Bounty amounts will be calibrated to reflect the logistics cost of moose habitat access (often remote, requiring longer trips than suburban tick monitoring). The first wave of moose-specific bounties is planned for late fall, timed to the period of larval attachment and the beginning of the body condition monitoring season.

**The longer-term vision:** If winter tick burden can be predicted from remotely-sensed climate and land cover inputs, the model can be run prospectively each fall based on temperature forecasts and early snowpack data. A seasonal early-warning tool — "this fall's tick burden in the White Mountains is predicted to be 40% above the 10-year average based on late snowpack forecast" — could inform moose hunting quota adjustments, targeted supplemental intervention deployment, and habitat management prioritisation in a way that is currently not possible.

This is the citizen-to-policy pipeline that citizen science exists to build.

## Program 2: Cyanobacteria Watch and Lake Health

### Why this, why now

Harmful algal blooms (HABs) caused by cyanobacteria — commonly called blue-green algae — are appearing earlier, lasting longer, and occurring in waterbodies that have no prior bloom history. Cyanobacteria produce cyanotoxins including microcystin (liver toxin), cylindrospermopsin (multi-organ toxin), and anatoxin-a (neurotoxin). Dog deaths after lake contact make local news every summer. Human illness is underreported because bloom-linked exposure is rarely traced by clinicians.

The underlying driver is lake eutrophication: excess nitrogen and phosphorus from agricultural runoff, suburban lawn fertilization, failing septic systems, and urban stormwater loading fuel algal growth. The eutrophication process is well understood, the contributing actions at the watershed level are identifiable, and several of them — rain gardens, riparian buffer planting, reduced fertilizer input, pet waste management — can be taken at the community level. The educational opportunity is to connect the bloom that people see and smell and fear to the watershed choices that produced it, and to give communities the tools to both monitor the problem and act on its causes.

### Educational unit: From Lawn to Lake — Tracking Eutrophication in Your Watershed

**Target audience:** Secondary school (grades 6–12), community lake associations, watershed councils, summer camp naturalist programs  
**Duration:** 3–6 sessions (works well as a springtime unit before summer bloom season)

**Core concepts taught:**

- **Nutrient cycle** — how nitrogen and phosphorus move from fertilizer application through soil and stormwater into waterways; why phosphorus binds to sediment and re-releases under low-oxygen conditions (internal loading)
- **Cyanobacteria biology** — what separates cyanobacteria from green algae (prokaryotic, fix atmospheric nitrogen, produce gas vacuoles that control buoyancy); why they outcompete other algae under high-nutrient, warm, calm conditions
- **Trophic state index** — oligotrophic (clear, low nutrients), mesotrophic (moderate), eutrophic (high nutrients, seasonal blooms), hypereutrophic (persistent blooms, oxygen depletion, fish kills). Participants learn to assess a waterbody's trophic state from Secchi disk depth, color, and odor before any chemical testing.
- **Bloom safety** — visual bloom identification (paint-like surface scum, pea soup color, blue-green ring around dried shoreline), odor (musty, sulfurous, grassy), when to close and how to report; why dogs are particularly vulnerable (groom fur after swimming, concentrate toxin)

**Core activities:**

- **Secchi disk construction and deployment** — groups build standardised 20cm diameter Secchi disks (black/white quadrant pattern), deploy from a dock or boat, record the depth at which the disk disappears and reappears. Simple, cheap, reproducible, and the measure used by state monitoring programs for decades. Secchi depth records from community lake groups are accepted by many state environmental agencies as supporting data.
- **CyanoScope observation** — participants photograph suspected bloom samples at defined magnification levels and submit via the CyanoScope app (EPA-developed) for AI-assisted identification and toxin risk categorization. Platform links CyanoScope submission to the site record.
- **Bloom reporting** — students learn to report blooms via their state's HAB reporting hotline (Massachusetts, New York, Connecticut, Vermont, Maryland, Virginia, and New Jersey all have active programs). Reporting contributes to the state's advisory system.
- **Nutrient source mapping** — on foot or via satellite imagery, participants map nutrient sources in the watershed: agricultural fields (identify crops and application timing), lawn areas within 100m of the shoreline, visible stormwater outfalls, septic system setbacks. The map becomes the basis for an action plan.
- **Rain garden as intervention** — connecting Unit 2 to the eco ops workflow: where can a rain garden intercept runoff before it reaches the lake? Groups design a conceptual placement using the nutrient source map they created.

**Learning outcomes:** Students can explain the eutrophication process from nutrient input to bloom formation, identify a cyanobacterial bloom by appearance and smell, deploy and interpret a Secchi disk, submit a bloom report to state monitoring, and identify at least one watershed-level action within the school's or community's control.

**Platform certificate:** `eco:citizen-science — Cyanobacteria Watch and Lake Health` — co-signable by school, lake association, state environmental agency, or university extension

## Program 3 (National): Freshwater Phenology and Stream Health

### The national thread connecting East Coast programs to a global network

Freshwater streams and rivers are phenological clocks: the emergence of aquatic insect larvae in spring, the timing of fish spawning runs, the appearance of specific macroinvertebrate families at specific seasons — these are among the most sensitive biological indicators of climate shift, water temperature change, and watershed disturbance that exist. And unlike many ecological phenomena, they can be monitored by a community member with a kick-net and a hand lens.

SCD Hub's **Freshwater Phenology Network** is a national citizen science track built around standardised macroinvertebrate sampling — the same BMWP (Biological Monitoring Working Party) biological index used in our Kenya and Costa Rica field programs. BMWP assigns tolerance scores (1–10) to macroinvertebrate families: stonefly larvae (Perlidae, score 10) require cold, clean, oxygen-rich water; tubificid worms (score 1) tolerate heavily polluted conditions. The index converts a handful of stream invertebrates into a quantitative water quality score that can be compared across sites and over time.

**The national aspect:** BMWP-style monitoring is standardised enough that data collected by a high school class in western Maryland, a community group on a Connecticut tributary, a tribal environmental office on the Upper Delaware, and a college student in coastal Maine can be directly compared. Aggregated across sites and years, the network produces the longitudinal dataset that is currently absent from the national freshwater monitoring picture — not because the methodology is difficult, but because the monitoring infrastructure to collect it at community scale has not existed.

**Connection to East Coast programs:**
- Tick Watch sites near streams can add a macroinvertebrate kick-sample to their seasonal visit at no additional cost
- Lake eutrophication monitoring extends naturally upstream: tracking macroinvertebrate communities in feeder streams identifies nutrient gradient and oxygen stress before the lake shows visible symptoms
- Rain garden installation sites can include a downstream reference stream for pre/post water quality comparison

**USA-NPN integration:** Participants are encouraged to register their monitoring sites with the USA National Phenology Network and submit parallel phenological observations: first leaf-out of riparian plants, first appearance of specific adult insects (stonefly, mayfly, caddisfly emergences are highly seasonal and temperature-dependent), ice-out dates for northern sites. This connects the macroinvertebrate biological record to the botanical and climate record in a single site log.

**Platform certificate:** `eco:citizen-science — Freshwater Macroinvertebrate Survey` — the same certificate issued in the Kenya and Costa Rica tracks, making Northeast US data directly comparable to global SCD Hub field sites

## How these programs connect to Wisdom from Elders

The most durable ecological data we can collect is the data that already exists in the memory of people who have been watching these waterbodies, forests, and fields for sixty or seventy years. An elder who grew up swimming in a lake that is now too toxic to touch in August is a primary source on the eutrophication timeline that no monitoring program can replicate. A farmer who remembers when blacklegged ticks were unusual this far north is a witness to a range expansion that academic drag-sampling can only partially reconstruct.

SCD Hub's Wisdom from Elders initiative — described in the Eco Ops Workflow Guide — is designed to integrate elder observation and traditional knowledge into the citizen science record as first-class data, not as anecdote. Participants in all three programs are encouraged to identify and invite elders with long-term knowledge of their monitoring sites as Knowledge Keepers, document their observations using our oral history protocol, and contribute those observations to the platform library alongside the biological monitoring data.

This is where citizen science becomes intergenerational science.
