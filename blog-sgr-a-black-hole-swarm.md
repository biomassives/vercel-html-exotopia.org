# The Swarm Around the Center

## What we actually know about the black holes gathering near Sagittarius A* — and what we corrected before publishing this

*SCD Hub / Exotopia.org — July 2026*

---

While building out the black hole observatory expansion (covered in a companion post), we set out to write something about a phenomenon we'd heard described as a "string of pearls" of smaller black holes being consumed by Sagittarius A*. We fact-checked it before writing a word of the actual post, and it's a good example of why that step matters.

## What didn't check out

"String of pearls" is a real astrophysics term — it just isn't about Sgr A*, and it isn't about black holes. It describes a 2014 finding (Swinburne University of Technology) of young star clusters strung out like beads around the supermassive black hole at the center of **NGC 2110**, a galaxy about 120 million light-years away in Orion. Simulations predicted that gas falling toward that black hole would fragment and form star clusters in a ring — "like beads on a string" — before tidal forces eventually pulled them apart and let them settle inward. It's a striking result. It just belongs to a different galaxy and a different kind of object than the one we were asked to write about.

We could have written the post anyway — the phrase is evocative, and nobody would have checked. We didn't, because this site cites Gillessen et al. and the GRAVITY Collaboration by name for its Sgr A* data, and shipping a post that misattributes a real finding to the wrong black hole would undercut every citation next to it.

## What actually is happening at Sgr A*

The real finding is arguably more interesting than the misremembered one, and it comes from much closer to home. In 2018, a team led by Charles Hailey used the Chandra X-ray Observatory to search the region within about a parsec (~3 light-years) of Sagittarius A* — a volume small enough that, until then, nobody had been able to resolve individual faint X-ray sources against the glare of the galactic center.

They found roughly a dozen X-ray binaries: quiescent systems in which a stellar-mass black hole is faintly, intermittently pulling material from a low-mass companion star. A dozen is a small number, but the region they searched is a small fraction of the galaxy's central few parsecs — and if a dozen are bright enough to detect in quiescence, the population that's too faint or too well-hidden to see at all is almost certainly much larger. Extrapolating from the detected sample, the Hailey team and follow-up theoretical work put the likely total at **10,000 to 20,000 stellar-mass black holes** concentrated in the galaxy's central few parsecs.

## How they get there

Stellar-mass black holes (roughly 5–30 M☉ — see our catalog entries for Cygnus X-1, the Gaia binaries, and the rest) form throughout the galaxy's disk and bulge, same as everywhere else. What's special about the region around Sgr A* is a slow sorting process called **dynamical friction**, sometimes described as gravitational mass segregation: in a dense stellar system, the heaviest objects gradually sink toward the center while lighter stars are stirred outward, in much the same way a heavy ball settles through a fluid of lighter particles. Over the roughly 10-billion-year age of the Milky Way's nuclear star cluster, black holes formed anywhere in the inner few hundred light-years have had time to spiral slowly inward and collect near the bottom of the gravitational well — around Sgr A* itself.

This is a genuinely different mechanism from anything at NGC 2110's "string of pearls." Nothing here is forming in a ring from infalling gas. It's older objects, formed independently over billions of years, sedimenting toward the same gravitational floor.

## Does Sgr A* actually consume them?

Occasionally, yes — but not on human timescales, and not violently in the way two neutron stars merging is violent. A stellar-mass black hole spiraling into a supermassive one is called an **extreme mass ratio inspiral (EMRI)**: gravitational-wave emission slowly bleeds orbital energy over millions of years, tightening the orbit long before any dramatic infall. These are exactly the sources the space-based **LISA** observatory (launching next decade) is built to detect — and the site's own Galactic Center scene already gestures at this. The IRS 13E object modeled there — an intermediate-mass black hole candidate about 0.1 light-years from Sgr A*, tidally stripping six Wolf-Rayet stars — is described in its own panel as "spiraling inward via dynamical friction — IMRI gravitational wave source for LISA." IRS 13E is plausibly a more massive, closer-in member of exactly the population Hailey's team found further out: not a string of pearls, but a slow accumulation of black holes at the bottom of the Milky Way's gravitational well, with a random few, occasionally, actually completing the trip.
