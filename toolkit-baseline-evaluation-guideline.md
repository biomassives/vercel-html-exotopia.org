*For group coordinators, facilitators, school partners, and scientific advisors*

## What This Guideline Is For

The Baseline Assessment document is a structured tool for understanding what a community group already knows before any training or lesson content is introduced. This guideline explains **how to use it** — who does what, in what order, and how to interpret what you find.

The guideline is written for four types of users. You do not need to read all sections — find your role below and follow the relevant path through the document.

| Your role | Sections to read |
|---|---|
| **Group coordinator** (e.g. Muirithi Jariffe) | Introduction, Roles, Steps 1–5, Facilitation Guide, Common Challenges |
| **External facilitator** (NGO, programme officer) | All sections |
| **School or university partner** | Introduction, Roles, Steps 3–4, Student Participation Notes |
| **Scientific advisor** | Introduction, Roles, Step 4 (Terms Intake), Scoring Guide, Quality Standards |

## Core Principles

Before the method, the principles. These govern every decision made during the evaluation.

**1. Community knowledge comes first.**
The baseline is not a gap-finding exercise that starts from what the community does not know. It starts from what they do know. Gaps are identified relative to the group's own goals and the work they want to do — not relative to a standard set by outsiders.

**2. Local terms are recorded exactly.**
When a community member uses a specific word or phrase for a plant, animal, or place, it is written down exactly as said. It is not corrected, translated, or normalised before the group has confirmed the entry. The local term is the authoritative entry. Scientific and Swahili equivalents are added later, alongside it — not instead of it.

**3. Depth of knowledge matters more than breadth of names.**
A group that knows three trees deeply — their fruiting seasons, the animals that depend on them, the soil conditions they prefer, the traditional uses of their bark and leaves — has more useful ecological knowledge than a group that can name forty trees without knowing what any of them do. The scoring rubric reflects this.

**4. At-risk knowledge is the highest priority.**
If a piece of ecological knowledge is held by one person in the group and has not been transmitted to anyone younger, it is at immediate risk of loss. These entries are flagged and prioritised for documentation above all other work, regardless of their score in other sections.

**5. The baseline is a baseline — not a judgement.**
Scores are reference points for planning. A low score in a domain does not mean the group has failed at anything. It means that domain is a candidate for support. A high score means the group is a candidate to teach, not to be taught.

## Who Does What — Roles in the Evaluation

A baseline evaluation works best when roles are clearly assigned before the process begins.

### Group Coordinator
The most important role. The coordinator knows the group members, understands local relationships, and holds the trust needed for honest answers. In the evaluation, the coordinator:
- Organises the baseline session (time, place, who attends)
- Introduces the purpose to the group in their own words and language
- Facilitates the group discussion in the Domain Inventory (Section 2)
- Collects and submits the local terms document (Section 3)
- Reviews the draft summary card (Section 7) before it is shared with any external party

The coordinator does not need to score the assessment alone. Scoring is done together with an external facilitator or, where none is available, with the group itself.

### External Facilitator
An NGO programme officer, Eco Ops field coordinator, or trained community liaison. The external facilitator:
- Brings the blank assessment document and explains each section
- Supports the coordinator during the Domain Inventory discussion
- Completes the scoring in Sections 2 and 5 during or immediately after the session
- Conducts the local terms intake (Section 3) from the document the coordinator provides
- Completes the Gap Analysis (Section 4) and Lesson Priority table (Section 6)
- Produces the Baseline Summary Card (Section 7)

Where no external facilitator is available, the coordinator and a trusted group member take on this role together.

### Scientific Advisor
A conservation biologist, botanist, ecologist, or university researcher. The scientific advisor's role in the baseline is specific and limited — they do not attend the community session unless invited. They:
- Receive the completed local terms intake table (Section 3) after the session
- Identify which terms have known Swahili or scientific equivalents
- Flag terms that do not match any known equivalent for follow-up
- Return a completed version of Section 3 within an agreed timeframe (recommend: 14 days)
- May optionally provide a short written note on any entries that are ecologically significant or surprising

### Student or School Partner
Students at a partner school or university programme. Students do not participate in the initial baseline session — that is a community process. After the baseline is completed, students:
- Receive the priority domain list and the local terms table
- Use iNaturalist and reference materials to help confirm or identify scientific names
- Contribute their own field observations to the group's Species Register as a follow-on activity

## Step-by-Step Process

### Step 1 — Prepare (Before the Session)

**Who:** Coordinator + External Facilitator

**Time required:** 1–2 hours

**What to do:**
1. Print the Baseline Assessment document, or open it on a device if the meeting location has power and visibility.
2. Read Section 2 (Domain Inventory) aloud together and agree on the twenty domains. Add any domain that is clearly relevant to the local context but not listed — write it in at the bottom of the table.
3. Agree on the facilitation approach: will the session be structured (one domain at a time, formal scoring) or conversational (open discussion, scoring done by the facilitator afterwards)? For groups with limited experience of formal assessment tools, conversational is usually better.
4. Prepare the local terms intake table (Section 3) separately — either as a printed sheet or a notebook page formatted as a simple two-column list (Local term | What it is). The full table can be filled in later.
5. Agree on who will take notes during the session.

**What to have ready:**
- Printed or on-screen copy of the assessment
- Notebook and pens
- A local terms sheet (simplified version of Section 3)
- Any existing group records — notebooks, WhatsApp photos, past reports — that might help fill in the group profile

---

### Step 2 — Open the Session

**Who:** Coordinator (leads), External Facilitator (supports)

**Time required:** 15–20 minutes

**What to say:**
The coordinator introduces the session in the group's primary language. The framing matters. Recommended opening:

> "Today we are not being tested. We are making a record of what this group already knows — about the land, the trees, the water, the animals around us. This record will help us decide what we need to learn, and also show others — schools, government, research partners — what knowledge we already have. What we know is valuable. We are writing it down so it cannot be lost."

Take time to answer questions before beginning. If any member is hesitant about information being recorded, clarify what will and will not be shared externally. The group has full control over the summary card (Section 7) before it goes anywhere.

**Complete the Group Profile (Section 1)** at the start of the session — it is quick and gives context for everything that follows. Read each field aloud and fill in the answers together.

---

### Step 3 — Domain Inventory Discussion

**Who:** Coordinator (facilitates), all group members (contribute), External Facilitator (scores)

**Time required:** 45–90 minutes depending on group size and knowledge depth

This is the core of the baseline session. Go through each domain in Section 2B.

**For each domain, ask three questions:**

1. **"What do we call the things in this domain?"**
   — Invite members to name species, habitats, or features. Write down every name offered, exactly as given. Do not filter.

2. **"What do we know about them?"**
   — For each named item, ask what it does, where it lives, when it changes, what eats it or what it eats, what happens when it is removed. Listen for relationships between species, not just isolated facts.

3. **"What have we done here?"**
   — Has the group planted, cleared, monitored, or restored anything in this domain? What was the outcome?

**While the discussion runs**, the external facilitator counts named terms and notes qualitative depth. Formal scoring (Coverage / Depth / Practical Capacity) is done by the facilitator immediately after the session while the discussion is fresh, not during the conversation itself. Scoring during the conversation interrupts the flow and can make members feel evaluated.

**Facilitation tips:**
- Start with the domains the group is most comfortable with (often: coastal trees, birds, soils). Confidence built early carries through the harder domains.
- If a domain produces silence, do not treat the silence as ignorance. Ask a more concrete question: "What trees do you see at the edge of the water?" rather than "What do you know about coastal forest?"
- If one or two members dominate, gently invite others: "Has anyone else seen something different?" Elders often hold the deepest knowledge but may wait to be specifically asked.
- When a member says "I know the old name for that but I have forgotten the Swahili" — write down the old name. That is an at-risk knowledge entry.

---

### Step 4 — Local Terms Intake

**Who:** Coordinator (provides document), External Facilitator (structures intake)

**Time required:** 30–60 minutes, may be done separately from the main session

The coordinator shares the local terms document they have collected. This may be a notebook, a list, a WhatsApp conversation, oral recall with a note-taker, or a formal written document.

**Intake process:**
1. Read each term from the document aloud.
2. Ask the group to confirm: is this the right word? Is it still used? Does everyone understand what it refers to?
3. Enter it in Section 3 exactly as confirmed by the group.
4. Ask one or two questions to establish depth: "What does it do?" "When do you see it?" "Is it common or rare now compared to when you were young?"
5. Assign a depth score (0–3) based on what is shared.
6. Note any term where the group's description does not match any species or habitat type the facilitator recognises — these are flagged in the Notes column.

**When the document is received after the session** (the usual case), the facilitator completes Section 3 from the document and returns a draft to the coordinator for confirmation before sending to the scientific advisor.

---

### Step 5 — Capacity and Readiness Assessment

**Who:** Coordinator + External Facilitator

**Time required:** 15–20 minutes

Work through Section 5 together at the end of the session or in a short follow-up conversation. This is straightforward — each question has a three-option answer (Yes / Partially / No, or equivalent). Read each question aloud and agree on the answer together.

The readiness assessment does not need to be discussed with the full group unless the coordinator prefers it. It is primarily a planning tool for the facilitator and coordinator.

---

### Step 6 — Complete Scoring and Gap Analysis (Post-Session)

**Who:** External Facilitator (primary), Coordinator (review)

**Time required:** 1–2 hours, done after the session

1. Assign Coverage, Depth, and Practical Capacity scores to each domain in Section 2B using the rubric and the notes from the session.
2. Complete the Gap Analysis (Section 4):
   - Identify the three strongest domains from the scores (Section 4A)
   - Identify the three highest-priority gaps (Section 4B)
   - Look for missing connections — domains where individual species are known but relationships between them are not (Section 4C)
   - Review all depth-score-3 entries and all session notes for at-risk knowledge — knowledge held by one person only (Section 4D)
3. Complete the Lesson Priority table (Section 6) based on the gap analysis.
4. Produce the Baseline Summary Card (Section 7).

Share the draft summary card with the coordinator for review before it is finalised or sent to any partner.

---

### Step 7 — Scientific Advisor Review

**Who:** External Facilitator (sends), Scientific Advisor (responds), Coordinator (receives results)

**Time required:** 14 days recommended turnaround

Send Section 3 (the completed local terms intake table) to the scientific advisor with a brief covering note explaining:
- The group's location and primary land use context
- The domains where most terms were collected
- Any flagged entries (terms with no known equivalent)

The advisor returns the table with:
- Scientific names filled in where they can be confirmed
- A note on any flagged term that may represent an undocumented local name for a known species
- A note on any flagged term that they cannot identify and needs further investigation (photograph, specimen, elder interview)

Entries that cannot be confirmed should not be removed from the register. They are marked as *unconfirmed* and remain in the record until resolved. An unconfirmed local name is still a genuine piece of knowledge.

## Scoring Guide with Worked Examples

### Coverage score — worked examples

**Score 0:** The group is asked about migratory birds. There is silence. One member says "I have seen some different birds in certain months but I don't know their names." → Score 0.

**Score 1:** The group names two migratory species — a stork and a kind of wader they see along the shore in specific months. They know when to expect them but not where they come from. → Score 1.

**Score 2:** The group names seven migratory visitors. They know the arrival month of four of them. → Score 2.

**Score 3:** The group names fifteen migratory species, distinguishes between those that stop briefly and those that overwinter, and notes that two species that used to come regularly have not appeared in three years. → Score 3.

---

### Depth score — worked examples

**Score 0:** "Mkoko. It is a tree that grows in the water." → Score 0.

**Score 1:** "Mkoko. It grows in salt water near the coast. The roots are above the mud." → Score 1.

**Score 2:** "Mkoko. The roots trap mud and fish hide in them when young. If you cut the mkoko the shoreline moves. We learned this when the trees were cut near the harbour — within two seasons the bank had shifted." → Score 2.

**Score 3:** "Mkoko. It flowers in the short rains and the crabs come at that time. The wood is very hard and was used for boat frames but that is now controlled. The seedlings float and plant themselves — you can see new trees starting at the mud edge each year after the rains. Our grandfathers said that where there is mkoko there will always be fish, and we have found this to be true." → Score 3.

---

### Practical capacity score — worked examples

**Score 0:** "We have not worked with mangroves directly." → Score 0.

**Score 1:** "We have mapped where the mangroves are in our area and photographed them." → Score 1.

**Score 2:** "We planted mangrove seedlings along a section of the creek three years ago. We do not know how many survived." → Score 2.

**Score 3:** "We planted mangrove seedlings along a 200-metre section in 2023. We visited three times and photographed the growth. About 70% survived the first dry season. We removed the ones that had died and replanted the gaps in 2024. The section now has good cover." → Score 3.

## Facilitation Guide — Session Formats

Different group contexts require different facilitation approaches. Choose the format that matches the group's meeting culture.

### Format A — Full group session (recommended for groups of 8–20)
- Duration: 2.5–3 hours including breaks
- Seating: circle or around a table — no front-of-room presenter position
- Materials: printed domain list, notebook for terms, pens, drinks
- Structure: Group Profile (15 min) → Domain Inventory (60–90 min) → Terms intake discussion (30 min) → Capacity assessment (15 min) → Close and next steps (15 min)
- Best for: established groups with a strong meeting culture; groups where discussion happens naturally

### Format B — Small group / key informant (for groups of 3–6, or for elder knowledge holders)
- Duration: 1.5–2 hours
- Seating: informal, conversational
- Materials: notebook only — no printed forms visible at first; introduce them gradually
- Structure: open conversation about the land and the work (30 min) → targeted questions on domains that emerge (45 min) → terms review (20 min) → close
- Best for: elder knowledge holders who may disengage from formal assessments; smaller leadership groups; follow-up sessions with specific members

### Format C — WhatsApp-based intake (for groups with limited in-person meeting time)
- Duration: 3–7 days (asynchronous)
- The facilitator sends one domain per day as a voice note or text message to the group WhatsApp
- Members respond with voice notes, text, or photos
- The facilitator compiles responses into the assessment document
- Best for: groups dispersed across a wide area; groups with very limited meeting time; situations where travel to a session is not possible
- Note: this format works well for the Terms Intake but is less reliable for the Domain Inventory, which benefits from group discussion and cross-checking between members

### Format D — Paper-first, digital-second (for low-connectivity contexts)
- The facilitator brings a printed copy of the assessment
- All entries are handwritten during the session
- The completed paper document is photographed and shared with the programme team
- The team transfers it to the digital assessment document
- Best for: contexts with no reliable internet; groups not comfortable with digital tools

## Common Challenges and How to Address Them

**"Members are reluctant to share knowledge because they worry it will be taken from them."**
This is a legitimate concern, especially regarding traditional knowledge and medicinal plant use. Address it directly at the start of the session. Clarify that: the record belongs to the group; the group controls what is shared with external partners; the Scientific Advisor review is specifically for adding scientific names — not for extracting community knowledge without attribution. Revisit the consent principle in Section 7: the coordinator reviews and approves the summary card before it goes anywhere.

**"Older members know a great deal but younger members seem uninterested or do not know the terms."**
Do not treat this as a problem with the younger members. It is the knowledge gap the platform exists to close. Record it in Section 4D as at-risk knowledge. The baseline itself is the first step toward solving it — making the elders' knowledge visible and credited is one way to open the transmission channel.

**"The group's scores are very low across most domains."**
First, check whether the facilitation reached everyone. Low coverage scores sometimes reflect facilitation failure (the wrong questions, the wrong format) rather than knowledge absence. If the facilitation was sound and scores are genuinely low, this is useful information. It means the group needs more foundational support than peer learning alone can provide and should be connected with a lesson track and a scientific advisor early. Low scores are not a disqualification from the programme — they are a signal about what kind of support to prioritise.

**"A member claims knowledge that other members dispute."**
Do not arbitrate in the session. Record the claim with a note indicating it is disputed within the group. Mark it for scientific advisor review. The value of the local terms register depends on it being honest — contested entries should be clearly marked rather than suppressed or accepted uncritically.

**"The local terms document contains names in multiple languages or dialects."**
Enter all of them. Record which language or dialect each term comes from. The register is richer for containing multiple naming traditions. The facilitator notes which terms are specific to one dialect so that the summary card does not present dialect-specific terms as universally shared.

**"The scientific advisor does not respond within 14 days."**
Do not delay the rest of the process. Proceed with the Gap Analysis and Lesson Priority table using the local knowledge already documented. Mark the Scientific Name column as *pending* and set a follow-up date. The assessment is usable without scientific names — it is richer with them, but the community knowledge it records has value independent of scientific confirmation.

## Student Participation Notes

For school or university partners receiving the baseline outputs:

The local terms table (Section 3) is the primary resource for student engagement. After the scientific advisor review, there will typically be a set of entries where the local name is known but the scientific name has not been confirmed. These are excellent iNaturalist research tasks — students search for the species using the description and photographs the group has provided and attempt identification.

Students do not correct the local terms. Their role is to add the scientific name column, not to revise what the community has documented. Any discrepancy between a community description and what students find in reference materials is noted and sent back to the group for discussion — not resolved unilaterally by the student.

If students visit the community for a field session, they come as learners from the community members before they contribute their own scientific knowledge. The framing for student visits: "We are here to see what you have shown us in the register."

## Quality Standards

A completed baseline meets quality standards when:

- [ ] Section 1 (Group Profile) is fully completed
- [ ] All twenty domains in Section 2B have been discussed and scored — no blank rows
- [ ] At least ten local terms are entered in Section 3 with depth scores
- [ ] All at-risk knowledge entries in Section 4D are identified with the name of the person who holds the knowledge
- [ ] Section 5 readiness scores are completed and reflect honest assessment, not aspirational answers
- [ ] Section 7 Summary Card has been reviewed and approved by the group coordinator before being shared externally
- [ ] The local terms table has been sent to a scientific advisor for name confirmation

A baseline that meets these standards is a reliable foundation for lesson planning, partner communication, and progress measurement. A baseline that does not meet them should be treated as a draft and completed before being shared.

## Revision Schedule

The baseline is not a one-time exercise. It should be repeated at the following intervals:

| Review point | What to update | Who leads |
|---|---|---|
| After first season (approximately 6 months) | Re-score Practical Capacity across all domains; add new terms to Section 3 from the season's observations | Coordinator + Facilitator |
| After first year | Full re-run of Sections 2, 3, and 5; compare to original scores; update Section 7 Summary Card | Coordinator + Facilitator |
| When group membership changes significantly | Update Section 1; check Section 4D for new at-risk knowledge entries | Coordinator |
| When a scientific advisor visit or workshop occurs | Add confirmed scientific names to any pending entries in Section 3 | Scientific Advisor |

Progress is measured as change from baseline — not against an external standard. The group's second-year baseline is compared to the group's own first-year baseline. This is the measure that matters.
