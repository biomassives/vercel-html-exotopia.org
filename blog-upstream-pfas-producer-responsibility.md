# Clean Compost from Source: An Upstream Producer Responsibility Toolkit for Community Advocates

*A practical guide for volunteers in the Nederland, Colorado zero-waste ecosystem — and any community running an in-vessel or open-windrow compost program that feeds community gardens. If you're planning this work before running it — deciding what to test, who to contact, and how it fits a broader campaign — see the companion strategy document, ["Designing a Municipal Compost Producer-Responsibility Campaign"](/blog/municipal-compost-campaign-strategy), first.*

---

## Why the problem starts upstream

The Greener Mountain EarthFlow composter at Nederland's waste transfer site does exactly what it's designed to do: it converts food scraps and organic material into finished compost. The problem is that PFAS — per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances — are chemically bonded into many of the paper and "compostable" packaging products that enter the organic waste stream alongside the food. The composter processes the organics but cannot remove the fluorine chemistry. At 55–70°C, PFAS compounds are thermally stable; they don't break down. In an in-vessel system, less leaching occurs during processing than in open windrows, which means PFAS concentrations in the finished product can be *higher*, not lower, than in the feedstock.

The finished compost goes to community gardens, home gardeners, and municipal plantings. PFAS moves from amended soil into plant tissue. Leafy greens — lettuce, spinach, kale, chard — accumulate the most. Root vegetables and fruiting crops take up less but are still measurable. Children eating produce from a contaminated community garden may receive doses substantially higher than from drinking water alone.

The solution is not to stop composting. It is to remove the contaminated materials before they enter the bin.

## What the contaminated materials look like

The largest category of PFAS contributors to municipal compost streams is **paper-based food service packaging treated with fluorinated grease barriers**. These are not obscure industrial products. They are items that land in the compost bin every day at every restaurant, café, school cafeteria, and food truck in town.

**High-suspicion items to audit:**

| Item | Why it's a problem | What to look for |
|---|---|---|
| Pizza boxes | Fluorinated grease barrier on inner surface | Inner surface repels oil; usually no label |
| "Compostable" cups (hot and cold) | PLA lining may be PFAS-treated; paper wall often has moisture barrier | BPI/OK Compost certification does not guarantee PFAS-free |
| Deli paper, sandwich wraps, butcher paper | Fluorinated coating to prevent grease soak-through | Waxy or glossy feel on one side |
| Paper plates (coated) | Moisture/grease resistant coating | Shiny surface; water beads immediately |
| Fast food bags and burger wrappers | Direct-food-contact fluorinated coating | Grease-resistant inner surface |
| Bakery tissue, parchment sheets | Silicone- or PFAS-coated release paper | Non-stick inner surface |
| Microwave popcorn bags | Historically PFOA; now short-chain PFAS still used | Interior metalised or coated layer |
| Paper straws (some brands) | PFAS used in some water-resistant coatings | Claim "compostable" but coating is fluorinated |

**"BPI certified compostable" does not mean PFAS-free.** The Biodegradable Products Institute updated their standard to prohibit intentionally added PFAS in 2021, but:
1. Products manufactured before the standard change are still in circulation and still being sold as stock is depleted
2. The standard prohibits intentional addition; it does not certify absence of PFAS from incidental contamination or fluorinated coatings on constituent materials
3. Some manufacturers self-certify without third-party verification

The **water drop test** is a useful first-pass screen: place three drops of water on the inner surface of the packaging. If water beads up and does not absorb within 30 seconds, the surface likely has a fluorinated or other hydrophobic coating. If it absorbs quickly, the material is more likely PFAS-free. This is a screening tool, not a laboratory result — but it is fast, cheap, and actionable in a field audit.

## The legal lever: Colorado HB 23-1034

Colorado enacted **HB 23-1034** in 2023. This is the upstream producer responsibility framework already in state law. Key provisions and implementation timeline:

- **2024:** Food packaging manufacturers must disclose PFAS content to Colorado retailers and distributors
- **2025:** Prohibition on PFAS in food packaging, food service ware, and food contact paper
- **2026:** Prohibition extends to additional consumer product categories

This means that **by 2025, no business in Colorado should be receiving new stock of PFAS-containing food packaging.** The advocacy task for 2024–2025 is:

1. Help businesses understand they have a compliance obligation
2. Help them identify which of their current products are likely non-compliant
3. Connect them with PFAS-free alternatives *before* they run out of current stock — rather than after, when they may face a rushed procurement decision

Frame this as a compliance assistance visit, not an accusation. Most food service operators have no idea their "eco-friendly compostable" packaging contains PFAS. The disclosure requirements in HB 23-1034 mean this information is now publicly available — many businesses simply haven't seen it yet.

## PFAS-free alternatives: what businesses can switch to

These brands have either independent third-party testing documentation or explicit PFAS-free declarations verified against product lines as of 2024:

**High confidence (independent testing documentation):**
- **World Centric** — declared PFAS-free across their line in 2021; independently tested and posted; good range of cups, plates, cutlery, take-out containers
- **Vegware** — third-party tested; strong range for café/restaurant settings
- **GreenPaper Products** — PFAS-free certified line; primarily cups and plates

**Good confidence (manufacturer declaration, recommend requesting SDS/test data):**
- **Eco-Products** (now Novamont-owned) — PFAS-free on most lines; verify specific SKUs, as legacy products may still be in distribution
- **StalkMarket** — bagasse (sugarcane fibre) containers; naturally PFAS-free if uncoated

**Always safe without checking:**
- **Unlined kraft paper bags** — if no grease-resistance coating, naturally PFAS-free
- **Sugarcane/bagasse trays and containers with no added coating** — PFAS-free by material
- **Reusable food service containers** (stainless, glass, uncoated ceramic) — eliminates the issue entirely

**Reuse programs as the cleanest solution:** A community deposit-and-return container program eliminates the compostable packaging issue altogether. Several Front Range cities (Denver, Boulder) have piloted these. Nederland, as a small mountain community with high dwell time per customer, is actually a strong candidate for a local reuse network. Cat's relationship with local businesses and the waste transfer site are the exact institutional anchors such a program needs.

## The business engagement playbook

### Phase 0: Research before you visit
- Visit the business as a customer first; photograph or note any compostable packaging in use
- Run water drop test on packaging you receive
- Check if the business already participates in Boulder County's green business program (if so, they may have a sustainability contact)
- Check Colorado DEQ's HB 23-1034 disclosure database once live (2025)

### Phase 1: The friendly audit visit

Come in person, during a slow period (mid-morning or mid-afternoon). Bring:
- A printed copy of the volunteer one-pager (see companion document)
- A list of PFAS-free alternatives for the specific packaging types you've identified
- A copy of HB 23-1034's compliance timeline for food service operators
- Your contact info and the project contact (Cat, Nederland sustainability)

Opening: *"Hi — I'm a volunteer with the Nederland zero-waste programme working alongside the town's sustainability manager. We're helping local businesses get ahead of Colorado's new packaging requirements. Do you have five minutes?"*

You are not there to inspect or accuse. You are there to help a business avoid a compliance problem and, as a side effect, clean up the compost stream. Most businesses will be receptive to this framing.

### Phase 2: Document what you find

Use the field audit form (available as a bounty template on the SCD Hub platform) to record:
- Business name, address, date of visit
- Packaging items observed / audited
- Water drop test results for each item
- Current supplier
- Owner/manager name and their level of awareness of HB 23-1034
- Agreed follow-up action (if any)

This data feeds into the platform's compost input tracking system and creates a documented record that Cat and the town can use when reporting to the county.

### Phase 3: Alternatives and recognition

Send a follow-up email within a week with:
- Specific product links for PFAS-free alternatives matching their packaging needs
- Pricing comparisons (World Centric and Vegware are typically cost-competitive)
- Contact info for their packaging distributor (often the distributor will have already transitioned their line by 2025 under HB 23-1034)
- Note that the Nederland zero-waste programme will list participating businesses publicly

Consider a **"Clean Compost Partner" window decal** for businesses that complete the switch — visible community recognition costs almost nothing and signals commitment to both locals and visitors.

## The community compost testing and improvement cycle

This is the ongoing technical backbone that volunteer advocacy feeds into:

**Step 1 — Baseline test (now):**
Send a sample of finished compost from the EarthFlow system to a lab offering the PFAS-40 panel — Eurofins, ALS Environmental, TestAmerica are all available in Colorado. Use ASTM D8421 for solid matrix. Cost: approximately $200–350 per sample. This establishes what you're working with.

**Step 2 — Input stream audit (months 1–3):**
Volunteer teams work through the business audit process above, documenting and photographing the packaging entering the compost stream. Prioritise: restaurants with heavy take-out volume, the school cafeteria, any food trucks operating during summer/winter seasons.

**Step 3 — Targeted input substitution (months 3–9):**
Work with businesses to switch their highest-PFAS items. Focus on volume first — one pizza restaurant making ten deliveries a night contributes more pizza box contamination than a boutique bakery making three. Track confirmed switches.

**Step 4 — Re-test compost (month 12):**
Re-sample finished compost using the same protocol as the baseline. Compare results. Calculate reduction in total PFAS load. This is the data that:
- Validates the advocacy work quantitatively
- Earns eco:certificates for participating volunteers through the SCD Hub bounty system
- Provides evidence for the town's reporting to Boulder County and the Colorado Composting Council
- Can be submitted to Colorado DEQ as community monitoring data

**Step 5 — Publish and repeat:**
Transparent publication of results — good or bad — builds community trust in the compost programme. A results card posted at the waste transfer site and shared with participating businesses closes the feedback loop and invites continued participation.

## Distribution gate: protecting gardens while the process runs

While the advocacy and testing cycle is underway, finished compost should be distributed with a **tiered use policy** based on available testing data:

| PFAS level (total, PFAS-40 panel) | Recommended use |
|---|---|
| < 2.5 ppb (Maine DEP agricultural trigger) | Suitable for food gardens — label tested |
| 2.5–10 ppb | Food gardens: avoid leafy greens; fruiting crops and root veg with caution; ornamentals OK |
| > 10 ppb | Hold for non-food uses: road revegetation, erosion control, ornamental beds, tree planting |
| Untested | Label "not yet cleared for food gardens — test in progress" |

**If PFAS is found above threshold:** Activated carbon amendment (biochar) can reduce plant-available PFAS in soil — roughly 10–15% carbon by volume reduces plant uptake by 30–60% in research settings. This is an interim tool, not a fix. The permanent fix is clean inputs.

## Connecting this to the SCD Hub platform

This work generates exactly the kind of structured field data the platform is built for:

- **Bounties:** Post specific audit tasks — "Complete business packaging audit at [business name], document packaging types, run water drop tests, submit field form" — as claimable bounties worth eco:certificate credits or small payments
- **Eco:certificates:** Volunteers who complete verified audits earn eco:certificates documenting their contribution to community environmental health. These are real, verifiable credentials with ZK-proof chain-of-custody.
- **Data feed:** Business audit records, compost test results, and packaging switch confirmations feed into the community's x₁ (land stewardship) and x₂ (water/watershed) E8 capability axes — meaning the Nederland community's profile in the Λ₂₄ lattice improves as the work is done.
- **Knowledge commons:** Successful alternatives, audit scripts, and test result comparisons are published as community resources, available to any other mountain town in Colorado running a compost programme.
