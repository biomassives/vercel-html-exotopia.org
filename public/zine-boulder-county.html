The problem
The work is real.
The credit isn't.

Community groups around the world — watershed councils, youth science groups, smallholder farmers — are doing irreplaceable environmental monitoring. Water quality. Soil health. Species counts. Tick surveillance.

Most of this work goes unrecognized, unpaid, and disconnected. A school in Vermont and a youth group in Kenya may be running identical stream health protocols. They have no idea the other exists. Neither receives a credential for the work. Neither can easily share what they've learned.

SCD Hub / Exotopia is a platform designed to fix all three at once:

The platform is open source, uses open data standards (GBIF, EPA ECHO, iNaturalist), and routes income directly to the people doing the work — not to a platform middleman.

Who's already using it: Community groups in Lamu County (Kenya), Vermont river monitoring networks, Costa Rica biodiversity projects, and Front Range Colorado citizen science groups. The tools work offline — no data plan required in the field.

Platform overview
An exoplanet settlement is a strange home for a watershed council. It works anyway.
Four things the platform provides, one visual metaphor holding them together
🌱
Eco:Certificates
Verifiable digital records of real field work, issued and checkable today — no wallet required. On-chain anchoring (Algorand) is roadmap, not yet live.
🏆
Bounty System
Specific monitoring tasks, posted publicly, claimed by community members. Payment is arranged manually today. Planned: pluggable, third-party-maintained connectors — M-Pesa, Algorand, Celo, Hbar, and others — so groups run their own payment infra on terms that fit their country and context.
📚
Training Library
Video curriculum for ecological monitoring, vocational skills (carpentry, wiring, EMT, accounting), and citizen science protocols. Works offline.
🌌
Settlement Map
Each community gets a navigable "exoplanet settlement" — a visual home for their data, achievements, and identity in the global network.
Why exoplanets? The settlement is a persistent, visual identity for a real community. It holds their monitoring records, certificates, and connections. It's also a useful fiction: communities 300 km apart can be "neighbors" on the same planet if they're working on the same ecological problem.

⚠ PFAS / PFOS — Two active fronts in Boulder County
Rocky Flats Legacy Site
Groundwater contamination persists for Louisville, Superior & N. Jefferson County. EPA Method 533 chain-of-custody guides and ECHO submission pathways are in the bounty system — free to use.
Nederland · Active collaboration
Working with Cat (Town of Nederland sustainability manager) on zero waste education + a PFAS testing regime for the waste transfer site's Greener Mountain EarthFlow 20-yd in-vessel composter.
⚠ Garden contamination pathway — direct human exposure risk
• PFAS moves from amended soil into plant tissue. Leafy greens accumulate highest: lettuce, spinach, kale, chard. Root vegetables (carrots, beets) and fruiting crops (tomatoes, squash) take up less — but still measurable.
• Short-chain PFAS (PFBS, PFHxA) are most mobile in soil and most readily taken up by roots. Long-chain PFOS/PFOA bind to soil organic matter — but still transfer. No chain length is safe to ignore in a food garden.
• In-vessel systems run at 55–70°C. PFAS are thermally stable — composting does not destroy them. It concentrates them: less leaching during processing means more retained in finished product.
• Colorado HB 23-1034 (2023) restricts PFAS in food packaging 2024–2026 — this reduces future inputs. But existing feedstock contamination requires testing now, before compost is distributed.
Testing + mitigation steps for Nederland
1. Test finished compost before any distribution to food gardens — ASTM D8421 solid matrix or Maine DEP PFAS-40 panel (Eurofins, ALS, TestAmerica all offer this). Bear Creek → South Boulder Creek watershed downstream.
2. Establish hold threshold: Maine's agricultural trigger is 2.5 ppb total PFAS. If exceeded, hold compost for non-food uses (roads, revegetation, ornamentals) only until source streams are cleaned up.
3. Upstream Producer Data Mapping: Trace food packaging and compostable supply chains back to regional distributors and manufacturers. Leverage Colorado's EPR framework (HB 22-1355) and PFAS restrictions (HB 23-1034) to track non-compliant feedstocks before they hit local digesters.
4. Interim mitigation if contamination found: activated carbon soil amendment sorbs PFAS before root uptake; avoid growing leafy greens in affected plots; increase soil organic matter to compete with PFAS for root absorption sites.
For the curious
The math behind the metaphor is as real as it gets.
A glimpse at the framework — full writeup at exotopia.org/blog

Every community in the network has a profile across eight capability dimensions — the same eight that appear in our human rights and Rights of Nature framework. These eight dimensions map onto a mathematical structure called E8, one of the most symmetric objects in mathematics. "Near" communities in E8 space are working on analogous problems and have things to teach each other.

The Leech Lattice (Λ₂₄)
Three stacked E8 blocks form a 24-dimensional structure called the Leech lattice. A community's full address in this space encodes: what it currently achieves (Block 1), what its ecosystems need (Block 2), and how it relates to time and network (Block 3). Parallel "universe" simulations for what-if scenarios are different addresses in the same 24D space.
◦ Roadmap — not yet built
🔐
Field work certificates are planned to use PLONK/halo2 zero-knowledge proofs — the same cryptographic infrastructure used in Ethereum's rollup ecosystem. The goal: certificates verifiable by anyone with the public inputs, without revealing the field worker's identity, with E8 root vectors as tamper-resistant identity slots. The spec is written; the circuit isn't built yet.
Live today: certificates are issued and verifiable in our database now — no wallet or cryptography knowledge required to earn one.
