# Designing a Municipal Compost Producer-Responsibility Campaign

*A strategy framework — for any community running or receiving municipal compost, not just one
town. Companion to ["Clean Compost from Source"](/blog/upstream-pfas-producer-responsibility),
which is the field-execution toolkit for the business-audit and testing-cycle work this document
plans around.*

---

## What this document is, and isn't

"Clean Compost from Source" is a tactical toolkit: water drop tests, a business audit script, a
list of PFAS-free packaging brands, a five-step testing cycle — written for one town's one
composter. It answers *how do I run the audit visit*.

This document answers a different question: *how do we decide what to test, who to talk to, and
in what order, before any audit visit happens* — and it's written so a community whose "municipal
compost" problem looks nothing like Nederland's (a food-scrap in-vessel composter) can still use
it. There are two genuinely different PFAS-in-compost pathways, and conflating them wastes a
campaign's first six months.

## The two pathways — figure out which one you actually have

**Pathway A — packaging-derived, in food-scrap/yard-waste compost.** PFAS enters via
fluorinated-coating food packaging (pizza boxes, "compostable" cups, deli paper) mixed into the
organics stream. This is what the Nederland toolkit covers. The fix is upstream substitution:
businesses switch packaging, less PFAS enters the bin. A single municipality can meaningfully move
this number by working through its own restaurants and cafés.

**Pathway B — biosolids-derived, in land-applied "biosolids compost."** Many municipal "compost"
or soil-amendment programs are built on treated sewage sludge (biosolids), not food scraps. PFAS
concentrates in biosolids because wastewater treatment doesn't remove it — it just moves fluorine
chemistry from wastewater into sludge. This is a bigger, more institutionally embedded pathway than
packaging: it runs through your wastewater utility's permit, not through restaurant supply chains,
and the fix is a completely different lever (industrial pretreatment programs, not a "switch your
cups" conversation).

**Do this first:** call your public works or utilities department and ask directly — is the
compost/soil-amendment product your municipality distributes made from biosolids, food scraps and
yard waste, or a blend? The answer determines almost everything downstream: who you test, who you
educate, and which regulatory lever you're pulling.

## The bigger picture: this is one instance of a much wider health-policy shift

*This section draws directly on Safer States' analysis,
["Beyond PFAS: class-based approach for toxic chemicals and plastics"](https://www.saferstates.org/resource/2026-analysis-of-state-policy-addressing-toxic-chemicals-and-plastics/beyond-pfas-class-based-approach-for-toxic-chemicals-and-plastics/)
— summarized and adapted here for a compost-campaign audience; read it directly for the full
analysis and current figures.*

It's worth situating a local PFAS-in-compost campaign inside the broader movement it's actually
part of, both because it's genuinely bigger than one contaminant and because the framing itself is
useful borrowed language.

**How we got here.** Over a decade ago, states began phasing out toxic chemicals — certain
bisphenols, phthalates, and others — in consumer products, starting with products for children. In
the past five years, that effort has expanded well past children's products into cosmetics,
menstrual products, and packaging generally. Alongside that, states have increasingly reframed
plastics themselves as a **health** issue, not solely a waste/litter issue — the same shift this
document is asking a compost campaign to make (PFAS-in-compost is a health exposure pathway, not a
diversion-rate problem).

**Why the underlying harms matter for public education (Phase 3 below).** The chemical and plastics
industry is projected to keep growing, and the health evidence behind this legislative wave is not
abstract:

- Plasticizers such as phthalates and bisphenols — still widespread in plastics, building
  materials, food packaging, and even food itself — have been linked to infertility, cancer, and
  developmental harm.
- Recent studies have detected microplastics in human reproductive organs, raising real concern
  about fertility and sperm quality.
- Toxic flame retardants remain common in electronics and building insulation despite evidence of
  health harm and real doubt about their actual fire-safety benefit — and they get recycled
  onward into packaging and utensils, which is directly relevant to a compost-input audit.
- Industrial solvents, including ones in ordinary household products, pollute indoor air and
  drinking water.

None of this is specific to PFAS — it's the same "toxic-chemical-in-everyday-products" pattern PFAS
is one instance of, which is exactly why a compost campaign's public materials can honestly connect
to this larger, well-evidenced concern rather than needing to justify PFAS alone as an isolated
worry.

**A stronger policy template than disclosure-then-ban: identify safer alternatives.** Most of the
packaging-law timeline in this document (Colorado's HB 23-1034, Maine's, Illinois's) follows a
disclosure-then-prohibition pattern — useful, but reactive. Washington has gone a step further,
using its existing regulatory authority not just to restrict a harmful chemical but to formally
identify safer replacement chemistry as part of the same evaluation process. That's a materially
better ask for Phase 4 advocacy than "ban X" alone: it puts the burden of finding what's safe to
switch to on the regulator/producer side, rather than leaving every downstream business or
municipality to research alternatives itself (which is exactly the gap the Nederland toolkit's
brand list exists to fill in the meantime).

**Check whether your state is already moving.** At least 18 states are expected to consider toxic
chemical and plastics policy this year: Alaska, California, Georgia, Hawaii, Illinois, Maryland,
Massachusetts, Maine, Minnesota, North Carolina, New Jersey, New York, Oklahoma, Pennsylvania,
Rhode Island, Utah, Vermont, and Washington. If your state is on this list, there is very likely
already a live bill or docketed rulemaking to plug a local compost campaign into — check your state
legislature's current session before assuming you'd be starting from zero.

## Why this is a live moment to organize around (not a someday issue)

A campaign lands better with a real, dated hook than with "PFAS is bad, eventually something
should happen." Two are currently live:

- **12+ states have now enacted PFAS-in-food-packaging restrictions**, several taking effect in
  2026 itself — Maine enforces a ban across nine plant-fiber food-contact packaging categories
  starting May 25, 2026; Illinois's law took effect January 1, 2026; Vermont, New Jersey, and
  others are at various stages; Kansas and New Mexico follow in 2027. If Pathway A is your issue,
  your state is very likely already somewhere on this timeline — check where.
- **EPA published a draft risk-reduction guidance for PFOA/PFOS in biosolids on July 1, 2026**, with
  a public comment period open through **September 4, 2026**. If Pathway B is your issue, this is
  a genuine, concrete, near-term action: submitting a public comment (as an individual, or as an
  organized community response) is real advocacy with a real deadline, not a symbolic gesture.
  Separately, several states have already enacted their own biosolids-PFAS rules (Washington twice
  over, Rhode Island, Oregon, Wisconsin, Connecticut), and Pennsylvania has proposed a tiered
  land-application framework (unrestricted below 20 ppb PFOA/PFOS, reduced application 20–100 ppb,
  prohibited at 100 ppb or above) worth studying as a template even if your state hasn't adopted
  one yet.

Both of these are dated, verifiable, and moving *while you're reading this* — treat every specific
date above as something to re-check against your state's current guidance before quoting it in
your own materials, not as settled.

## The five-phase campaign framework

### Phase 1 — Map your local pathway (weeks 1–2)

Answer the Pathway A/B question above. Then map who actually has authority over what you'd want
changed: a public works director (biosolids permit, wastewater pretreatment program), a waste
transfer site manager (food-scrap composter, business relationships), or both. This is the same
"who do I actually contact" research the map-research step of Exotopia's ecology citizen-science
workflow already formalizes — use it here even if this isn't nominally an "ecology site": the
contact-pathway table (municipal facility → department → the right first phone call) is the same
kind of homework either way.

### Phase 2 — Build the evidence base (months 1–3)

Before advocating for anything, get a baseline number. For Pathway A, that's a finished-compost
PFAS panel from an accredited lab (the Nederland toolkit's Step 1 — a PFAS-40 panel via ASTM
D8421, roughly $200–350/sample, is the same baseline test regardless of which town you're in). For
Pathway B, it's usually already being tested somewhere in your wastewater utility's permit
paperwork — request that data before commissioning new testing; several states (New York among
them) are moving toward requiring biosolids PFAS sampling and public result reporting, so the data
you need may already be collectible via a records request rather than a fresh lab bill.

Either way, **use a published threshold framework to interpret the number**, don't just report a
raw ppb figure. Maine's agricultural-use trigger (2.5 ppb total PFAS, cited in the Nederland
toolkit) and Pennsylvania's proposed tiered framework (20 ppb / 100 ppb) are two real, citable
reference points — pick whichever is closer to your own state's guidance, and say explicitly which
one you're using and why.

### Phase 3 — Public education, two audiences at once

**Residents and gardeners** need a plain answer to "is it safe to grow food in this?" — not a
chemistry lecture. Lead with the tiered-use table from the Nederland toolkit (adapted to your own
test result and whichever threshold framework you picked in Phase 2), and be explicit that testing
in progress is a normal, responsible state to be in, not a reason for alarm.

**Businesses** need the compliance-assistance framing the Nederland toolkit already establishes for
Pathway A: most food-service operators have no idea their packaging contains PFAS, the relevant
disclosure/prohibition law is public information they likely haven't seen yet, and a friendly
audit visit that ends in "here's a PFAS-free alternative and here's the price" gets far more
uptake than an accusatory one. If you're working Pathway B instead, the equivalent audience is
industrial dischargers into the sewer system (the businesses whose effluent actually contributes
PFAS to biosolids) — same non-accusatory framing, different door to knock on.

### Phase 4 — Turn evidence into producer-responsibility advocacy

This is where a campaign stops being "we tested our compost" and becomes "we're pushing for the
upstream fix." Concretely:

- If your state already has a packaging-PFAS law (most now do, in some form) but no enforcement
  visibility yet, your test results and business-audit records are exactly the kind of documented,
  dated evidence that gives a local health department or state agency something concrete to act on
  — publish them, don't just file them.
- If your state has no biosolids-PFAS rule yet, Pennsylvania's tiered framework and Washington's
  phased sampling-then-reporting timeline are both real, currently-live templates a local advocacy
  group can point to and say "adopt something like this."
- The EPA comment period closing September 4, 2026 is open to anyone, not just registered
  organizations — a community that has done Phase 2's testing work has genuinely useful firsthand
  data to contribute to that comment record, not just an opinion.

### Phase 5 — Institutionalize and share

A one-time test-and-report cycle fades. Publish results transparently (good or bad), re-test on a
schedule, and — this is the Exotopia-specific piece — feed the structured data into the platform's
citizen-science tooling rather than a one-off PDF that only your town ever sees again:

- Log the site and the testing/advocacy project in the **PFAS Citizen Science** tool
  (`/pfas-citizen-science`) — a `decon_project` with real logged progress entries is exactly this
  campaign's Phase 2–4 work, made visible to every other community using the platform, not just
  yours.
- If your pathway turns out to be more ecology/watershed-shaped than decon-shaped (e.g. downstream
  water-quality monitoring near a biosolids application site), the parallel **Ecology & Biodiversity**
  tool (`/ecology-citizen-science`) and its access-status pipeline (map research → letter of
  inquiry → confirmed access) is the right home instead — use whichever matches what you're
  actually doing, not both by default.
- If your evidence supports a specific remediation or packaging-substitution method others could
  adopt, publish it as a **method proposal** (`/method-proposals`) with real citations — this is
  how one town's Phase 4 work becomes another town's Phase 1 starting point.

## First 30 days — a concrete starting checklist

1. Call public works/utilities: is your municipal compost Pathway A, B, or both? (Phase 1)
2. Request existing biosolids PFAS test data via records request if Pathway B, or price a
   PFAS-40 panel if Pathway A. (Phase 2)
3. Pick a threshold framework (Maine's 2.5 ppb, Pennsylvania's tiered 20/100 ppb, or your own
   state's if it has one) and commit to using it consistently in everything you publish.
4. Read the [Nederland toolkit](/blog/upstream-pfas-producer-responsibility) in full if you're
   working Pathway A — it has the actual audit script.
5. If Pathway B and no state rule exists yet, read EPA's July 2026 draft guidance and calendar
   September 4, 2026 as a firm deadline to submit a comment.
6. Create the project in Exotopia's PFAS or Ecology citizen-science tool so the work is logged
   from day one, not reconstructed from memory at month six.
